The steps

  1. Introduction. A senator or representative introduces a bill; many are prefiled in December. The Legislature meets once a year, from the second Monday of January until it adjourns "sine die" in the spring or early summer. Bills that haven't passed by then die.
  2. Committee. The Speaker or the Senate President assigns the bill to one or more standing committees. Each chair decides whether it gets a hearing; the committee votes "do pass" (DP) or "do pass amended" (DPA).
  3. Rules, caucus and the floor. Each house's Rules Committee checks every bill, the party caucuses discuss it, and the full house debates and amends it as the Committee of the Whole before voting on third reading.
  4. The other house. The bill starts again in a committee of the other house. If that house amends it, the first has to agree and pass it again ("final read"), or a conference committee works out the differences.
  5. The governor. During the session the governor has 5 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto a bill; after the session adjourns, 10 days. The governor can veto items in a spending bill. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house.
  6. When it takes effect. New laws take effect 91 days after the session adjourns, unless passed as emergency measures by two-thirds of each house.
  7. Constitutional amendments. The Legislature proposes an amendment to the state constitution in a concurrent resolution (HCR or SCR). It goes through committees and both houses like a bill and needs a majority of the members of each house, but it doesn't go to the governor: it goes on the ballot at the next general election, in November of an even year, and takes effect only if the voters approve it. (The Legislature can also send laws to the voters this way, and citizens can put amendments on the ballot by petition; neither is shown here.)

How to have an impact

  • Sign in on a bill. Through the Legislature's Request to Speak system you can register for or against a bill before a committee hearing, or ask to testify.
  • Ask the committee chair. A bill moves only if the chair puts it on the agenda. Write the chair and members, and ask your own legislators to talk to them.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state senator and two state representatives, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor a bill and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to introduce it again next year.

About this data

Where the data comes from: Open States, which publishes the Arizona Legislature's bills, actions, votes and legislators as public-domain data, updated daily; committees and Capitol phone numbers from the Legislature's website, azleg.gov; and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for places and counties. (The Legislature's own data service asks automated readers not to use it.)

House and Senate bills of the regular session are shown, and the concurrent resolutions proposing constitutional amendments (which resolutions those are comes from their official titles, read from azleg.gov); other resolutions, laws referred to the voters, memorials and special sessions are left out. Topics come from the committees that acted on a bill and the words in its title, so a bill can have more than one.

There are no plain-language summaries in the data; each bill's page on azleg.gov has its text and the Legislature's fact sheets. Votes list how each member voted.

Governor's deadlines are estimates from the Constitution's rule (5 days during the session, 10 after it, Sundays excepted).

The steps

  1. Introduction. The Legislature meets once a year, from the Tuesday after the first Monday of January: 90 days, or 125 in the first year of a four-year term (2024, 2028). A member files a bill, drafted by the legislative staff. Bills that don't pass by the end of the session die, and most die sooner, at a deadline.
  2. Committee. The Speaker refers House bills, and the Lieutenant Governor Senate bills, to a standing committee on their subject; many go to two (a policy committee, then Appropriations, Ways and Means or Finance). The committee reports the bill "title sufficient, do pass", often with amendments or a substitute, or doesn't take it up. A bill that isn't reported by the committee deadline dies there ("Died In Committee"), as most do.
  3. Floor. A reported bill goes on its house's calendar and has to pass by the floor deadline, or it dies on the calendar. Most bills need a majority; bills that raise revenue or issue bonds need three-fifths, and a few need two-thirds. A member who voted with the majority can enter a motion to reconsider, which holds the bill in that house until the motion is decided; a bill held that way past the deadline dies.
  4. The other house. The bill goes through committee and the floor again, with its own deadlines. If the second house amends it, the first house agrees ("concurs") or asks for a conference committee; a conference report has to be adopted by both houses, or the bill dies in conference.
  5. The governor. The bill is enrolled, signed by the Speaker and the Lieutenant Governor, and sent to the governor, who has 5 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto it while the Legislature is in session, or 15 days after it adjourns. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. The governor can veto parts of an appropriation bill. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house. Laws are chapters of that year's session laws.
  6. Constitutional amendments. The Legislature proposes an amendment to the Mississippi Constitution with a concurrent resolution (HC or SC). It skips the governor: two-thirds of each house send it to the voters.
  7. The calendar. Most laws take effect on July 1, when the state's budget year starts, unless the bill sets another date.

How to have an impact

  • Get the bill taken up before the committee deadline. Most bills die because their committee never reports them, and the first deadline comes about four weeks into the session. Ask your legislators and the bill's sponsors to press the chair, and write the committee members yourself.
  • Know who refers bills. The Speaker and the Lieutenant Governor decide which committee gets each bill, and a bill sent to two committees has to clear both by the deadline.
  • Move quickly. Each step has a deadline, so a bill that stalls for a week or two can die.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to sign on as an author and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the author to file it again next January; bills are often tried several years running.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Mississippi Legislature's bill status system (billstatus.ls.state.ms.us): each bill's history, authors, committees and chapter number, and the members' pages (districts, parties, phones, committees); and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, towns, villages and counties.

House and Senate bills of each regular session are shown, with the concurrent resolutions that propose constitutional amendments; other resolutions and extraordinary sessions are left out. Appropriation bills are marked "Budget bill", and local and private bills (for one city or county) are counted apart in comparisons. Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and words in its title, so a bill can have more than one.

The data has no summaries of what bills do: each bill's title is its short title, and the text under it is the act's official title. How each member voted is published only as scanned roll-call sheets (PDFs), which aren't read here, so the site shows no votes; each bill's link leads to them.

Legislators' details are the current members' only. "Where you live" uses the Census Bureau's 2024 district maps; a few districts redrawn by court order for the November 2025 special elections may differ, so check with your address if you live near a district line.

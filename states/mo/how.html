The steps

  1. Introduction. The General Assembly meets once a year, from early January to mid-May. Members can prefile bills from December 1. No bill can pass after 6 p.m. on the Friday in mid-May set by the Constitution, and bills that haven't passed by then die.
  2. Committee. The Speaker refers House bills to committees, and the President Pro Tem refers Senate bills. A committee holds a public hearing and then votes in an executive session, often on a committee substitute ("HCS" or "SCS"); a bill it doesn't vote out dies there. A House bill voted out of its standing committee goes to one of the House's Rules committees for a second vote, and bills that cost the state more than $100,000 go to Fiscal Review (House) or Fiscal Oversight (Senate).
  3. Floor. Each house votes twice. First the bill is "perfected": debated, amended and approved, usually by voice. Then it's "third read and passed" by a recorded vote of a majority of the house's members (82 representatives, 18 senators). Bills nobody opposes can go on a consent calendar. In the Senate, a filibuster can hold a bill until the session runs out.
  4. The other house. The bill goes through a committee and both floor votes again; the second house often replaces it with a substitute ("SS for HCS for HB 2"). If it changes the bill, the first house has to adopt the changes and pass it again, or ask for a conference committee, whose report both houses have to adopt before passing the bill once more. When both houses have passed the same text, it's "truly agreed to and finally passed."
  5. The governor. The bill is signed by the Speaker and the President Pro Tem and delivered to the governor, who has 15 days to sign or veto it, or until mid-July (45 days after May 30) for bills passed at the end of the session; a bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law without a signature. The governor can veto items in an appropriation bill. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house's members (23 senators, 109 representatives), usually at the veto session in September. Most new laws take effect August 28.
  6. Constitutional amendments. Amendments to the Constitution of Missouri are joint resolutions (HJR, SJR). A majority of each house's members sends one to the voters at the next general election, or at a special election the governor calls, never to the governor for a signature.

How to have an impact

  • Get the bill heard and voted out. Most bills that fail never get a committee vote. Ask your legislators and the bill's sponsor to press the committee chair, and write the committee members yourself.
  • Testify. Committees take public testimony at their hearings in the Capitol in Jefferson City; the House and the Senate post each week's hearings, and witnesses fill in a witness form. Written testimony can go to the committee.
  • Get it to the floor. In the House, a bill voted out still needs a Rules committee and then the Majority Floor Leader to put it on the calendar; in the Senate, the Majority Floor Leader decides. Ask your legislators to press for it.
  • Move early. The session ends in mid-May, and the last weeks are crowded: bills that haven't passed one house by spring rarely make it.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state senator and one state representative, and they listen most to their own constituents. Senators take messages through a contact form on the Senate's site.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask a legislator to prefile it for next session (from December 1).

About this data

Where the data comes from: the House's XML exports (House bills with every action in both houses, sponsors and co-sponsors, the House's subject index, committee and floor tallies; representatives and House committees), the Senate's website (Senate bills, their actions and the Senate's bill summaries; senators and Senate committees), the Senate Journal (how each senator voted), and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities and counties.

House and Senate bills of each regular session are shown, with the joint resolutions that propose constitutional amendments; concurrent and simple resolutions and extraordinary (special) sessions are left out. Appropriation bills are marked "Budget bill". A bill combined into another ("HCS for HBs 594 and 508") ends there, with a line saying which bill went on. Topics come from the committees a bill went to and, for House bills, the House's subject index.

"What it does" is the Senate's summary of a Senate bill's current version. The House's summaries (PDFs) aren't read yet, so House bills show only their one-line description.

Votes: how each senator voted on a bill's final passage comes from the Senate Journal, matched by day and bill (a few journal days aren't downloaded yet). How each representative voted is published in the House Journal, which isn't read here, so House floor votes are tallies only. Committee tallies are the House's; Senate committee votes aren't shown.

Laws are bills the governor signed (budget bills often with items vetoed), bills that became law without the governor's signature, and bills passed over a veto. No bill was vetoed outright in 2024, 2025 or 2026.

The Senate publishes no email addresses: senators are reached through its contact form. A past year's senators who have since left are listed by name only.

The steps

  1. One house. The Council of the District of Columbia has 13 members: one for each of the 8 wards and 5 elected at large, by the whole city, one of them the Chairman. No party can hold more than two of the four at-large seats besides the Chairman's, so two of them usually go to independents. Each Council Period lasts two years (Council Period 26 is 2025–26), and the Council meets all year, with a summer recess. A bill that hasn't been enacted when the period ends on December 31 of the even year dies.
  2. Introduction. A Councilmember, or the Chairman at the Mayor's request, introduces a bill (numbered B26-0001 and so on; this site writes B 1). The Chairman refers it to a committee, sometimes to two, one after the other.
  3. Committee. The committee holds a public hearing, then may mark the bill up: vote on its own version and a report. Most bills never get a mark-up.
  4. Two readings. A bill the committee approves goes to the Committee of the Whole (all 13 members, with the Chairman presiding), which puts it on a legislative meeting's agenda. The Council then votes on it twice, at a first reading and a final reading at least 13 days later. Each passes with a majority of those voting; many bills pass on the consent agenda, with no debate.
  5. The Mayor. The act goes to the Mayor, who has 10 days, not counting Saturdays, Sundays and holidays, to sign or veto it. An act the Mayor doesn't return in time is enacted without the signature. The Council can override a veto within 30 days with two-thirds of the members present and voting (9 of 13 when all vote). In the budget, the Mayor can veto single items.
  6. Congress. Every act then goes to Congress, because the District isn't a state. It becomes law after 30 days of Congress's session (60 for changes to criminal law), which often takes two to four months, unless Congress passes, and the President signs, a resolution disapproving it. This site counts an act as enacted when the Mayor signs it (or it's enacted without the signature, or over a veto), as laws are counted in every state, and shows it as under review by Congress until it gets its law number (L26-0001 and so on).
  7. Emergency and temporary acts. Because the review takes months, the Council often passes two short-lived versions of a bill alongside the permanent one. An emergency act passes in one reading, once two-thirds of the members vote that it's an emergency; it takes effect as soon as the Mayor signs it, skips Congress, and lasts 90 days. A temporary act goes through both readings and Congress's review and lasts 225 days, covering the time until the permanent law is in force. Each has its own bill number. This site lists only permanent bills; where a permanent bill has emergency or temporary versions, its history names them and what became of them.
  8. The budget. The Mayor proposes the budget each spring, and the Council votes on it, with its Budget Support Act, in May and June. The District's budget also goes to Congress, which appropriates it as part of the federal budget.

How to have an impact

  • Testify at the hearing. Anyone can testify at a committee's public hearing, in person, by video, or in writing; written testimony goes in the record. Hearings are announced in the D.C. Register a few weeks ahead (each bill's history says when a notice was published).
  • Ask for a mark-up. Most bills die in committee without a vote. Ask the committee's chair to mark it up, and the bill's introducer to push for it.
  • Write your Councilmembers. You have six: your ward's member and the five elected at large. All of them vote on every bill at its two readings.
  • Write the Mayor once the Council has passed a bill: the Mayor has 10 working days to sign or veto it.
  • Write again only with news: a new fact, a local impact, a new stage.
  • Ask others to write too. Ten people writing once counts for more than one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your neighborhood carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the introducer to introduce it again in the next Council Period.

About this data

Where the data comes from: LegiScan, which collects the Council's bills, histories, sponsors, roll-call votes and members from the Council's Legislative Information Management System (LIMS) and publishes them under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license; bill data on this site is from LegiScan, updated weekly and more often while the Council meets. (LIMS asks automated readers not to use it.) Acts and laws, with their numbers and effective dates, also come from the Council's own law library (code.dccouncil.gov, published as open data), which fills in the last steps LegiScan's records stop before. Councilmembers' and committees' details come from dccouncil.gov; neighborhoods and wards from the District's open data.

Only permanent bills are shown. Emergency and temporary acts, about half of the Council's bill numbers, are left out, and named in the history of the permanent bill they go with when their titles match; resolutions are left out. Topics come from the committee a bill went to and the words of its title, so a bill can have more than one.

"What it does" is the abstract the Council's Secretary writes for each bill as introduced, with the enacted act's long title from the law library. Votes at both readings, and on overriding a veto, are shown member by member; votes on amendments and motions are left out. LIMS records no committee votes.

Stages are worked out from each bill's history. An act counts as enacted when the Mayor signs it, it's enacted without the signature, or the Council overrides a veto; it becomes law after Congress's review. The Mayor's deadline is worked out from the day the act was sent (10 days, not counting Saturdays, Sundays and the District's holidays); each act's own due date, which LIMS gives, is in its history.

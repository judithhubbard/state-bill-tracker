The steps

  1. Introduction. The Legislature meets once a year for 60 days, from the second Wednesday of January (of February in the year a new governor takes office, as in 2025). Members can file bills before it opens. Bills that don't pass by the last day die.
  2. Committee. The Speaker or the Senate President refers each bill to a committee on its subject, and often to a second one ("To Health and Human Resources then Judiciary"); the second reference can be dispensed with. A committee recommends a bill ("do pass"), often as a committee substitute or with amendments, or never takes it up, which ends it. A committee can fold several bills into one substitute. Since 2025 the House's committees also work through subcommittees.
  3. Floor. A bill is read on three separate days, then voted on; four-fifths can suspend that rule. In the House of Delegates, the Rules Committee moves bills from the House Calendar to the Special Calendar, where they're taken up. Passing a bill takes a majority of the members present; making it take effect sooner than 90 days after passage takes a separate two-thirds vote.
  4. The other house. The bill goes through committee and the floor again. If the second house changes it, the first house has to agree ("concur") and pass it again, or the second house can drop its changes ("recede"); otherwise a conference committee tries to settle the differences, and both houses have to adopt its report.
  5. The governor. The bill is enrolled and presented to the governor, who has 5 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto it during the session, or 15 days (Sundays excepted) after the session ends; a bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law without a signature. The governor can veto or reduce items in an appropriation bill. A veto during the session can be overridden by a majority of each house's members (18 senators, 51 delegates; two-thirds for the budget and supplementary appropriation bills), or the Legislature can amend the bill to meet the governor's objections and pass it again. Most vetoes come after the session ends, and stand. Laws are numbered chapters of each year's Acts (Acts ch. 46); most take effect 90 days after they pass.
  6. Constitutional amendments. Amendments to the West Virginia Constitution are joint resolutions (HJR, SJR). Two-thirds of each house's members (23 senators, 67 delegates) send one to the voters, at the next general election unless the resolution sets a special one, never to the governor.

How to have an impact

  • Get the bill on an agenda. Most bills that fail are never taken up by their committee. Ask your legislators and the bill's sponsor to press the committee chair, and write the committee members yourself.
  • Speak at a public hearing. Committees can hold public hearings on bills, and anyone can sign up to speak; written comments can go to the committee's staff.
  • Get it to the floor. A recommended bill still has to be read on three days and, in the House, put on the Special Calendar. Ask your legislators to press for it.
  • Move quickly. The session lasts 60 days, and the 50th day ("crossover day") is the last for a bill to pass its own house (budget bills excepted).
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one delegate and two state senators, and they listen most to their own constituents.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask a legislator to introduce it again next session.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Legislature's bill status pages for each regular session (each bill's short title, sponsors, text versions, subjects and history; the lists of completed bills and of the governor's actions; the roll-call votes; the drafters' notes in the bills' texts), its member and committee pages, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for places and counties.

House and Senate bills of each regular session are shown, with the joint resolutions that propose constitutional amendments; concurrent and one-house resolutions and special sessions are left out. Appropriation bills are marked "Budget bill". Many bills each year authorize state agencies' rules; several are often folded into one bill. Topics come from the committees a bill went to and the Legislature's subjects, so a bill can have more than one.

"What it does" is the drafter's note at the end of the bill's text ("The purpose of this bill is ..."), from the newest version that has one; Senate committee substitutes often leave it out, and then the note describes the bill as introduced.

Floor votes on passing each bill are shown member by member, from the roll calls. Members' votes are tied to today's legislators for 2025 and 2026; for 2024, only to members who also served in 2025, and others appear by name. Committee votes aren't published by member.

Laws are bills with a chapter in that year's Acts: signed by the governor or law without the governor's signature. They match the governor's lists of signed and vetoed bills and the Legislature's list of completed bills, bill for bill.

Sponsors are matched to today's members by name; sponsors who have left the Legislature are shown as the record names them (by surname).

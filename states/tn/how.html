The steps

  1. Introduction. Each General Assembly lasts two years (the 114th is 2025–26) and meets each year from the second Tuesday of January, usually until late April. A bill is filed in both houses at once: a House Bill with a representative as sponsor and a Senate Bill with a senator, with the same text. Bills stay alive through both years and die when the General Assembly ends.
  2. Committee. Each version goes to a committee in its own house. In the House it goes first to a subcommittee, then to the full committee; in the Senate, to a standing committee. Committees vote to recommend a bill, often with amendments, or let it sit: the sponsor can take it "off notice", and a Senate committee can send it to its general subcommittee or to summer study, where bills usually stay. Bills that cost money also go to Finance, Ways and Means, where they wait "behind the budget" until the budget passes.
  3. Floor. The House's Calendar and Rules Committee and the Senate's Calendar Committee put bills on a floor calendar (the consent calendar for bills nobody objects to). A bill passes on third consideration with a majority of the house's members: 50 in the House, 17 in the Senate.
  4. The other house. When one version passes its house, the other house takes it up in place of its own companion bill ("substitutes" it) once its own version is ready for the floor, and votes on it. If the second house amends it, the first house has to agree; if they disagree, a conference committee writes a report both houses have to adopt.
  5. The governor. The Speakers of the Senate and the House sign the bill and it goes to the governor, who has 10 days, Sundays excepted, to sign or veto it. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law without a signature, even after the session ends. The governor can reduce or strike items in spending bills. Overriding a veto takes a majority of each house's members. Laws are numbered as public chapters ("Public Chapter 78"); acts for one city or county are private chapters.
  6. Constitutional amendments. The General Assembly proposes an amendment to the Tennessee Constitution with a joint resolution (HJR or SJR). It skips the governor: it has to pass by a majority of each house's members in one General Assembly and by two-thirds in the next, and then the voters decide at the next election for governor.
  7. The calendar. Most new laws take effect July 1; some take effect when signed or on a date the bill sets.

How to have an impact

  • Get the bill on a calendar. Most bills die in subcommittee or committee without a vote. Ask your legislators and the bill's sponsors to push for a hearing, and write the committee's members.
  • Mind both versions. Every bill has a House number and a Senate number. Both have to clear their own house's committees before the bill can pass, so follow the version in each house.
  • Testify or write the committee. Committees meet in the Cordell Hull Building in Nashville; meetings are streamed online and calendars are posted the week before.
  • Move quickly. Sessions are short: most votes happen between February and late April.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to sign on as a co-sponsor and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to take it up in the second year, or to file it again in the next General Assembly.

About this data

Where the data comes from: LegiScan, which collects the Tennessee General Assembly's bills, histories, sponsors, roll-call votes and legislators and publishes them under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license; bill data on this site is from LegiScan, updated weekly, and more often while the General Assembly meets. (The General Assembly's own website asks automated readers not to use it.) Who holds each seat now, with party and email, comes from Open States' public-domain list of legislators; Capitol phone numbers from LegiScan; cities, towns and counties by district from the Census Bureau's district relationship files.

House and Senate Bills of the regular sessions are shown, with the joint resolutions that propose constitutional amendments; other resolutions and extraordinary (special) sessions are left out. A bill's House and Senate numbers are shown together as one bill, and the stage is the furthest either reached; each number keeps its own history. Topics come from the General Assembly's subject index and the committees a bill went to, so a bill can have more than one.

“What it does” is the bill summary the General Assembly writes, as LegiScan publishes it. It says which version it describes: “As introduced” or “As enacted”, so a bill amended along the way may differ from it. Votes are shown member by member. The data has no committee members or chairs, so contact cards don't name a committee's chair.

Stages are worked out from each bill's history. Governor's deadlines are estimates: 10 days, Sundays excepted, after the bill is sent.

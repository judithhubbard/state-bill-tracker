The steps

  1. Introduction. A member, or a committee at the request of a state agency, introduces a bill. The Legislature meets each year from the second Tuesday of January, for 40 legislative days in odd years and 35 in even years (into mid-March), then returns for one day near the end of March to consider vetoes. Bills that don't pass by then die.
  2. Committee. The Speaker or the Senate President sends the bill to a committee on its subject. Every bill gets a hearing, where anyone can testify. The committee votes to pass it ("do pass"), or kills it by deferring it "to the 41st legislative day" (there are only 40) or tabling it. Bills that spend money often go to the Appropriations Committee.
  3. Floor. A bill the committee passes goes on the calendar; uncontested bills go on the consent calendar and pass together. A bill needs a majority of all members: 36 in the House, 18 in the Senate (two-thirds for one with an emergency clause or a special appropriation).
  4. The other house. The bill goes to the other house and starts again in committee. If the second house amends it, the first has to agree ("concur"), or a conference committee works out the differences.
  5. The governor. During the session, the governor has 5 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto a bill; bills that arrive at the end get 15 days after adjournment. The governor can veto items in an appropriation bill. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. On veto day, overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house.
  6. Constitutional amendments. The Legislature proposes an amendment to the South Dakota Constitution with a joint resolution (HJR or SJR). It goes through committees and both floors like a bill and needs a majority of each house's members, but it skips the governor: it goes to the voters at the next general election in November of an even year. Citizens can also put amendments on the ballot by petition.
  7. The calendar. Most new laws take effect July 1; a bill with an emergency clause takes effect when signed.

How to have an impact

  • Testify at the hearing. Every bill gets a committee hearing, where anyone can speak in person or, for many committees, remotely; the Legislature's website lists each committee's agenda.
  • Move quickly. The session is short, and most bills that die are deferred to the 41st day at their first hearing.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state senator and two state representatives (one in districts 26 and 28), and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor a bill and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to introduce it again next session.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Legislature's data service, the same one sdlegislature.gov uses (each bill's actions, sponsors, subjects and votes; the members and committees), updated daily, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, towns and counties.

House and Senate bills of the regular sessions are shown, with the joint resolutions that propose constitutional amendments; other resolutions, commemorations and special sessions are left out. Topics come from the Legislature's subject keywords and the committees a bill was sent to, so a bill can have more than one.

There are no plain-language summaries in the data; a bill's title is its own one-line description. Votes list how each member voted, in committee and on the floor.

Governor's deadlines are estimates from the Constitution's rule (5 days during the session, Sundays excepted, and 15 days after it).

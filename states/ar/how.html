The steps

  1. Introduction. A member files a bill, drafted by the Bureau of Legislative Research. The General Assembly meets in a regular session in odd years, from the second Monday of January (60 days, usually extended into April), and in a fiscal session in even years, from the second Wednesday of April (30 days), which is for the appropriation bills that make up the state's budget; any other bill needs two-thirds of each house to be introduced then. Bills that don't pass by the end of the session die.
  2. Committee. The bill is read twice and referred to a standing committee; appropriation bills go to the Joint Budget Committee, with members of both houses. The committee can recommend it "do pass" (often after amending it), "do not pass", or send it to an interim committee for study. A bill the committee never takes up dies there.
  3. Floor. A bill reported "do pass" goes on its house's calendar for a third reading and a vote. It needs a majority of all members: 51 in the House, 18 in the Senate. An emergency clause, which makes a law take effect at once, takes two-thirds; many appropriations and most tax increases take three-fourths.
  4. The other house. The bill goes through committee and the floor again. If the second house amends it, the first house has to agree ("concur") before it can go on.
  5. The governor. The bill is enrolled and sent to the governor, who has 5 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto it while the General Assembly is in session, or 20 days after it adjourns. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. The governor can veto items in an appropriation bill. Overriding a veto takes a majority of each house's members. Laws are numbered as Acts (Act 123 of 2025).
  6. Constitutional amendments. The General Assembly proposes an amendment to the Arkansas Constitution with a joint resolution (HJR or SJR). It skips the governor: a majority of each house's members sends it to the voters at the next general election. The General Assembly can refer up to three amendments per election.
  7. The calendar. Laws take effect on the 91st day after the session adjourns, unless the bill has an emergency clause or sets another date.

How to have an impact

  • Get the bill taken up in committee. Most bills that fail are never taken up by their committee. Ask your legislators and the bill's sponsors to press the chair, and write the committee members yourself.
  • Speak at the committee meeting. Committees hear from the public at the State Capitol; meetings are streamed online and agendas are posted ahead.
  • Move quickly. Sessions are short, and a fiscal session takes up almost nothing but the budget.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to sign on as a co-sponsor and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsor or an interim committee to take it up before the next regular session.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Bureau of Legislative Research's data files for the Arkansas General Assembly (each bill's history, sponsors and act number; the members and committees), with the Legislature's website (arkleg.state.ar.us) for the 2024 fiscal session, which the files don't cover, and for how each member voted; and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, towns and counties.

House and Senate bills of the regular and fiscal sessions are shown, with the joint resolutions that propose constitutional amendments; other resolutions and extraordinary sessions are left out. Appropriation bills are marked "Budget bill". Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and words in its title, so a bill can have more than one.

The data has no summaries of what bills do: each bill's title is its official subtitle. Members' votes are filled in as the Legislature's roll-call pages are read; committee votes aren't published.

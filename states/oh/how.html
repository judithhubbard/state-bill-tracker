The steps

  1. Introduction. A member of the House or Senate introduces a bill. Each General Assembly sits two years, from January of an odd year (the 136th is 2025–26), and its bills stay alive until it ends December 31 of the second year.
  2. Committee. The bill goes to a committee on its subject, which usually holds several hearings: the sponsor's testimony, then supporters ("proponents"), opponents and others, and votes on whether to report it. Bills that spend money often go on to the Finance Committee.
  3. Rules and Reference. Each house's Rules and Reference Committee, chaired by the Speaker or the Senate President, schedules bills for floor votes.
  4. Floor. The full house votes. A bill needs a majority of all members: 50 in the House, 17 in the Senate.
  5. The other house. The bill goes to the other house under the same number and starts again in committee. If the second house amends it, the first has to agree ("concur"), or a conference committee works out the differences.
  6. The governor. The governor has 10 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto a bill, and can veto items in a spending bill. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. Overriding a veto takes three-fifths of each house.
  7. The calendar. The Legislature meets year-round, with the two-year operating budget due by June 30 of the first year. Most new laws take effect about 90 days after the governor signs them.

How to have an impact

  • Testify at a hearing. Ohio committees hear from supporters ("proponents"), opponents and other interested people, usually in writing submitted before the hearing. A bill usually has several hearings, so there's often time.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor a bill and to vote for it.
  • Ask the chair for a hearing or a vote. A committee's chair decides which bills come up; the leaders decide which reach the floor.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to introduce it again in the next General Assembly.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Legislative Service Commission's legislative data service (each bill's versions, sponsors and actions with every vote; the members and committees), updated daily; legislators' email addresses from Open States, which copies them from the House and Senate sites; and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, villages and counties.

House and Senate bills are shown; resolutions are left out. Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and the words in its title, so a bill can have more than one.

“What it does” is the purpose stated in the bill's official title. Votes list how each member voted, in committee and on the floor.

Governor's deadlines are estimates: 10 days, Sundays excepted, after a bill reached the governor.

The steps

  1. One house. Nebraska's Legislature has a single house of 49 senators, one from each district, elected without party labels; there's no second chamber to pass a bill again. Each Legislature lasts two years (the 109th is 2025–26): a 90-day session from January of the odd year to early June, and a 60-day session from January of the even year to mid-April. Bills carry over from the first session to the second; any bill still pending when the second session ends is indefinitely postponed.
  2. Introduction. Senators and committees may introduce bills only during the first 10 legislative days of each session (later with the Legislature's consent). A bill that spends money gets a separate appropriation bill, its “A bill” (LB 304A pays for LB 304), which moves with it.
  3. Committee. The Reference Committee (the Executive Board) sends each bill to one of 14 standing committees, and every bill gets a public hearing. The committee then advances it to General File, often with its own amendment, or kills it by indefinitely postponing it. Most bills simply stay in committee.
  4. Priority bills. Each senator may name one priority bill a session, each committee two, and the Speaker up to 25 (the Speaker's “major proposals” too). The Speaker sets the agenda, and with far more bills than floor time, priority bills are the ones that get debated.
  5. Three rounds on the floor. General File is the first round of debate: senators amend the bill and vote to advance it. Select File is the second round, with more amendments. Final Reading is the vote that passes it: 25 votes, or 33 for a bill with an emergency clause (which takes effect at once). Debate is limited: after about eight hours on General File (fewer on later rounds), ending a filibuster takes a cloture motion with 33 votes. When cloture fails, the bill is set aside, usually for the year.
  6. The governor. The Speaker and the presiding officer sign the bill and it goes to the governor, who has 5 days, Sundays excepted, to sign or veto it; a bill the governor doesn't return becomes law. The governor can veto items in an appropriation bill. Overriding a veto takes three-fifths of the senators, 30 of 49. Laws without an emergency clause take effect three calendar months after the session adjourns.
  7. Omnibus bills. Senators often amend one bill's text into another (“provisions of LB 80 amended into LB 814”), so a bill can die on its own while what it does becomes law in another. Each bill's history says where its text went.
  8. Constitutional amendments. The Legislature proposes an amendment to the Nebraska Constitution with a resolution (“LR 19CA”) that passes on Final Reading with three-fifths of the senators, 30 of 49. It skips the governor and goes to the voters at the next general election, or at a special election the resolution sets.

How to have an impact

  • Testify at the hearing. Every bill gets a public hearing, usually in January, February or March. You can testify in person in Lincoln or send written comments to the committee before the hearing; they go in the record.
  • Ask for a priority. A bill without a priority designation rarely reaches the floor. Ask its introducer, the committee or your own senator to name it a priority bill.
  • Count to 33. A bill that draws a filibuster needs 33 senators to end debate. Ask your senator how they'll vote on cloture, not only on the bill.
  • Bring in your own senator. You have one state senator, who listens most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor the bill and to vote for it at each round.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just advanced from General File"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the introducer to take it up in the second session, or to introduce it again in the next Legislature.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Nebraska Legislature's website: each bill's page (its title, introducer and history), each recorded vote member by member, the senators' and committees' pages, and the Clerk of the Legislature's worksheets, the official tally of bills signed, vetoed and passed, which the laws and vetoes here match bill for bill. Cities, villages and counties by district come from the Census Bureau's district relationship files.

Bills (LB) and their appropriation bills (A bills) are shown, with the resolutions that propose constitutional amendments (LR ... CA); other resolutions are left out. A bills are tagged and left out of the statistics, since each only carries the money for another bill. Topics come from the committee a bill went to and the words of its title, so a bill can have more than one.

Nebraska's senators are elected without party labels, and the Legislature lists no party for them, so this site shows none: no party on senators' cards, and no statistics by party. The governor is elected with a party.

Floor votes are shown member by member for each round: advancing from General File and Select File, cloture motions to end a filibuster, passing on Final Reading, and votes to override a veto. Votes on amendments and other motions are left out. Committee votes are only in the committee statements (PDFs), which aren't read, nor are the introducer's statement of intent and the fiscal notes, so there's no summary of what each bill does: follow the link to the bill's page for its text and statement of intent. Co-sponsors are the senators the history shows adding their names. Only current senators are known by full name and district; former senators are shown by the surname the records use.

Stages are worked out from each bill's history. Governor's deadlines are estimates: 5 days, Sundays excepted, after the bill is presented.

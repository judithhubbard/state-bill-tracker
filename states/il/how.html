The steps

  1. Filing. A member of the House or Senate files a bill. Each General Assembly sits two years, from January of an odd year (the 104th is 2025–26), and its bills stay alive until it ends.
  2. Rules or Assignments. Every bill goes first to the House Rules Committee or the Senate Assignments Committee, which the Speaker and the Senate President control. It decides whether to send the bill to a committee for a hearing. Most bills never leave.
  3. Committee. The committee holds a hearing, where people can file witness slips saying whether they support or oppose the bill, and votes. Bills that miss the session's deadlines go back to Rules or Assignments.
  4. Floor. The full house votes, on second and third reading. A bill needs 60 votes in the House and 30 in the Senate; after May 31 of a year, a bill that takes effect before the next June needs three-fifths.
  5. The other house. The bill goes to the other house under the same number, where a member sponsors it, and starts again in Rules or Assignments. If the second house amends it, the first has to agree (“concur”).
  6. The governor. A bill that passes both houses goes to the governor within 30 days. The governor has 60 days to sign it, veto it, or send it back with changes (an “amendatory veto”). A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. Overriding a veto, or accepting the governor's changes, happens in the fall veto session; an override takes three-fifths of each house.
  7. Constitutional amendments (HJRCA, SJRCA) need three-fifths of the members of each house, and then go to the voters at the next general election (in even years) held at least six months later, not to the governor. The General Assembly can put amendments to no more than three articles of the Constitution on one ballot.
  8. The calendar. The spring session runs from January to May 31; the fall veto session is a few weeks in October and November. Most new laws take effect January 1.

How to have an impact

  • File a witness slip. Before a committee hearing, anyone can file a witness slip online saying whether they support or oppose a bill. Committees count them, and they're the easiest way to be heard.
  • Get it out of Rules. A bill still in the Rules or Assignments Committee needs the leaders' go-ahead. Ask your own legislators, and the chair of that committee, to move it.
  • Mind the deadlines. Each spring has committee and floor deadlines; a bill that misses one goes back to Rules, where it usually stays.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor a bill and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to file it again in the next General Assembly.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the General Assembly's public file repository (each bill's status, sponsors, synopsis and actions; the members and committees), updated nightly, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, villages and counties.

House and Senate bills and proposed constitutional amendments (HJRCA, SJRCA) are shown; other resolutions are left out. Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and the words in its title and synopsis, so a bill can have more than one.

“What it does” is the Legislative Reference Bureau's synopsis of the bill as introduced. Votes are the tallies in each bill's history; the files don't say how each member voted.

Governor's deadlines are estimates: 60 days after a bill reached the governor.

The steps

  1. Introduction. The Legislature sits for two years (2025–26 is the 33rd Legislature), with a session each year from the third Wednesday of January to early May. Most bills are introduced in the first week or two of each session, often with a twin in the other house. A bill that doesn't pass in the first year carries over to the second, where it left off; bills still not passed when the second session ends die.
  2. Committees. Each bill is referred to its committees when it's introduced, usually two or three in a row, sometimes two together. Each committee holds a hearing with public testimony and then votes: it recommends the bill be passed, often with amendments that make a new draft, or defers it, which usually ends it. Most bills are never heard. Bills that spend money end with the money committee: Finance in the House, Ways and Means in the Senate.
  3. Deadlines. Each session has deadlines set by the leaders: bills must clear their committees and pass their own house by early March, and get through the other house by early April.
  4. Floor. Each house votes on a bill three times; the last is the passage, by a majority of the members. Members can vote "aye with reservations", which counts as yes.
  5. The other house. The bill goes through the other house's committees and floor. If it passes unchanged, it goes to the governor. If it's changed, the first house agrees to the changes and passes it on final reading, or disagrees and a conference committee of members from both houses writes a final draft (CD 1), which both houses pass on final reading in the session's last days.
  6. The governor. For a bill sent in the session's last ten days or after it (most bills), the governor has until the 45th day after the session ends (Saturdays, Sundays and holidays aren't counted: mid-July), and by the 35th day has to tell the Legislature which bills the governor intends to veto (the intent-to-veto list). A bill not signed or vetoed by then becomes law without a signature. The governor can veto items in an appropriation bill. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house's members (17 senators, 34 representatives); the Legislature can meet in special session on the 45th day to do it. Laws are Acts, numbered each year ("Act 250 of 2025").
  7. Constitutional amendments › Amendments to the State Constitution are bills too ("Proposing an amendment to Article ..."). Two-thirds of each house's members on final reading in one session, or a majority of each house's members in two successive sessions, put one on the ballot at the next general election. The governor gets notice of it but has no veto.

How to have an impact

  • Get the bill a hearing. Most bills that fail are never heard by one of their committees. Ask your legislators and the bill's introducer to press the committee chair, and write the committee members yourself.
  • Testify. Every committee hearing takes written testimony, submitted online a day or more before the hearing, and most take testimony in person or by video. Committees count the testimony for and against.
  • Deadlines. A bill that misses decking or crossover usually waits until next year, or dies at the end of the second year.
  • Follow both versions. Many bills have a twin in the other house; either can be the one that moves.
  • Bills that cost money. A bill that spends money has to get through Finance and Ways and Means, and then be funded in conference.
  • Bring in your own legislators: they listen most to their own voters. Ask them to co-sponsor, to press the chair or leaders, and to vote for it.
  • Write the governor before the intent-to-veto list. Most bills reach the governor after the session; the list comes out in late June.
  • Write again only with news: a new fact, a local impact, a new stage.
  • Ask others to write too. Ten people writing once counts for more than one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.

About this data

Where the data comes from: Open States (public domain), which copies the Legislature's bill pages: each bill's title, description, introducers, history and committee votes. The Legislature's own website turns automated readers away, so this site uses Open States' copy; the links to the Legislature's site are for you to follow. Legislators' parties, districts and emails are from Open States' list of legislators; their office phones are from the Public Access Room's directory of the Legislature. Places are from the Census Bureau's district relationship files.

House and Senate bills of each two-year Legislature are shown, including bills proposing constitutional amendments; resolutions and the governor's messages are left out. A bill that carried over from the first year to the second is one record with its whole history. Topics come from the committees a bill went to, else words in its title, description and subject terms.

Each bill's title is the first sentence of its description, the Legislature's short summary of the current draft (the official titles say only "Relating to taxation"); the full description and official title are shown under it. Open States' copy has no other summaries, so "What it does" isn't shown. A description ending "Effective 7/1/3000" (or a similar far-off date) is a deliberately defective effective date, which committees add to keep a bill in play for conference.

Committee votes are shown member by member, from the histories (members voting "aye with reservations" count as yes). Floor votes on passage are shown member by member too: the histories name those voting no and those excused. The House's histories don't give the number voting yes, so it's counted as the House's 51 seats less those voting no and excused (one too many while a seat is vacant). Names are tied to today's members; members who have left are shown by surname only, and votes from 2023–24 are tied to a member only when that member also serves now. The histories don't name committee chairs, so committees are shown with the members who voted in them this year, without a chair.

Laws are bills the governor signed, let become law without a signature, or that passed over a veto; each gets an Act number. Counted by year, they match the governor's announcements: 2023, 263 laws (one of them, SB 921, over a veto) and 11 vetoes; 2024, 253 and 7; 2025, 313 and 8; 2026, 266 and 1.

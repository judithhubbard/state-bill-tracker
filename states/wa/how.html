The steps

  1. Introduction. A member of the House or Senate introduces a bill (or prefiles it in December). The Legislature works in two-year bienniums, a 105-day session in the odd year and a 60-day one in the even year, and bills stay alive through both.
  2. Committee. The bill goes to a committee on its subject, which holds a public hearing and votes. Bills that cost money also go to a fiscal committee: Appropriations or Finance in the House, Ways & Means in the Senate. Each session has deadlines ("cutoffs") for committees to act.
  3. Rules. A bill a committee approves goes to that house's Rules Committee, which decides which bills reach the floor. Bills it doesn't plan to move go in its "X file."
  4. Floor. The full house debates and votes. A bill needs a majority of all members: 50 in the House, 25 in the Senate.
  5. The other house. The bill goes to the other house under the same number and starts again in committee. If the second house amends it, the first has to agree (“concur”), or a conference committee works out the differences.
  6. The governor. During a session, the governor has five days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto a bill; bills that arrive at the end get 20 days after the session ends. The governor can veto whole sections of a bill. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house.
  7. Constitutional amendments (joint resolutions, HJR and SJR) need two-thirds of the members of each house: 66 in the House, 33 in the Senate. They don't go to the governor: the voters decide at the next general election, in November.
  8. The calendar. Sessions start the second Monday of January. Most new laws take effect 90 days after the session ends.

How to have an impact

  • Note your position at a hearing. Once a bill is on a committee's hearing agenda, anyone can sign in online to note support or opposition, testify in person or remotely, or send written testimony, up to an hour before the meeting.
  • Comment to your legislators. The Legislature's "comment on a bill" form sends your message to your district's three legislators, or call the Legislative Hotline at 800-562-6000.
  • Mind the cutoffs. Each session has deadlines for committees and for each house to pass bills. A bill that misses one usually waits for the next session, or dies at the end of the biennium.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state senator and two state representatives, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor a bill and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to introduce it again in the next biennium.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Legislature's web services (each bill's versions, history, sponsors and roll calls; the members and committees), its committee pages for chairs and members, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, towns and counties.

House and Senate bills and the joint resolutions that propose constitutional amendments are shown; other resolutions, memorials and the governor's appointments are left out. A substitute ("SHB 1003") is shown as a version of its bill. Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and the words in its title and description, so a bill can have more than one.

“What it does” is the House or Senate staff's bill report. Floor votes list how each member voted.

Governor's deadlines are estimates from the Constitution's rule (five days during a session, 20 after it, Sundays excepted).

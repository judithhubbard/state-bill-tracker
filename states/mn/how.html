The steps

  1. Introduction. Each Legislature lasts two years (the 94th is 2025–26) and meets each year: from January in odd years, when it writes the two-year budget, and from February in even years, until the Monday after the third Saturday in May at the latest. Most bills are introduced in both houses with the same text: a House File (H.F.) with a representative as chief author and its companion Senate File (S.F.) with a senator. Bills stay alive through both years and die when the Legislature's second session ends.
  2. Committee. Each version goes to a committee in its own house: first the committee on its subject, then, if it spends money or changes taxes, a finance committee, the Senate Finance or Taxes Committee, or the House Ways and Means or Taxes Committee. A committee approves a bill, often amended, and sends it on to the next committee or to the floor; it doesn't vote bills down, it just doesn't act on them. Many bills never move on their own but are folded into a large omnibus bill for their budget area. Committee deadlines in March and April (Joint Rule 2.03) end most bills' chances for the year.
  3. Floor. An approved bill goes on the House's General Register or the Senate's General Orders; leaders put bills on the House's Calendar for the Day or take them up as special orders. A bill passes on its third reading with a majority of the house's members: 68 representatives, 34 senators. Every passage is a roll call.
  4. The other house. When one version passes its house, the other house usually takes it up in place of its own companion ("substitutes" it), often amending it to match its own text first. If the second house changes the bill, the first house has to agree and pass it again; if they disagree, a conference committee of members from both houses writes a report, which both houses have to adopt and repass without changes.
  5. The governor. The Speaker and the President of the Senate sign the bill and it goes to the governor, who has 3 days, Sundays excepted, to sign or veto it; a bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. For a bill passed in the session's last 3 days, the governor has 14 days after adjournment, and a bill not signed by then doesn't become law (a pocket veto). The governor can veto items in an appropriation bill. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house. Laws are chapters of the session laws ("Laws 2025, chapter 39"), numbered through the Legislature's two years.
  6. Constitutional amendments. The Legislature proposes an amendment to the Minnesota Constitution with a bill that passes by a majority of each house's members. It skips the governor and goes to the voters at the next general election, where it needs a majority of everyone voting in the election: leaving the question blank counts as a no.
  7. The calendar. Most new laws take effect August 1; budget laws July 1, the start of the state's fiscal year; others when signed or on a date the bill sets.

How to have an impact

  • Get a hearing early. Most bills die in committee without a hearing, and the committee deadlines come in March and April. Ask the bill's authors and your legislators to push for a hearing, and write the committee's members.
  • Mind both numbers. Most bills have a House File and a Senate File. Each has to clear its own house's committees, so follow the version in each house.
  • Watch the omnibus bills. Much of what becomes law rides in a large omnibus bill for a budget area (education, health and human services, transportation, taxes ...). Ask the finance committee's chairs, and then the conference committee's members, to include your bill or leave it out.
  • Testify or write the committee. Committees meet in the Capitol complex in St. Paul; hearings are streamed online, and agendas are posted a few days ahead.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to sign on as an author and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the authors to take it up in the second year, or to introduce it again in the next Legislature.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Revisor of Statutes' bill status records (each bill's description, authors, actions in each house, companion and chapter number); the Minnesota House's floor votes, member by member, its members and committees, and House Research's bill summaries; the Minnesota Senate's journal (where its members' votes are), its members and committees, and the summaries by Senate Counsel, Research and Fiscal Analysis; and the Legislative Reference Library's list of vetoes, to check the totals. Cities and counties by district come from the Census Bureau's district relationship files.

House Files and Senate Files are shown, with the bills that propose constitutional amendments; resolutions are left out. Most of the 2025 budget passed in a one-day special session on June 9, 2025, whose bills are numbered from H.F. 1 and S.F. 1 again: they're shown here as HFS and SFS (HFS 2 is H.F. 2 of the 2025 First Special Session), and their laws are chapters of that special session ("Laws 2025, First Special Session chapter 3"). The regular-session omnibus bills whose content passed there instead stay where they died, with a line in their history naming the special session's bill; a few of those numbers carried a different law in 2026. A bill's House and Senate numbers are shown together as one bill, and the stage is the furthest either reached; each number keeps its own history. Topics come from the committees a bill went to and the words of its description, so a bill can have more than one.

“What it does” is the newest nonpartisan summary of either number, from House Research or the Senate's counsel and analysts, and says which version it describes; a bill changed later may differ from it. Bills without one show only the Revisor's one-line description. Floor votes are shown member by member: the House's from its vote records and the Senate's from its journal; members who voted that day but not on the bill are shown as not voting. Committee votes aren't in the records.

Stages are worked out from each bill's history. Governor's deadlines are estimates: 3 days, Sundays excepted, after the bill is presented, or 14 days after the session for bills passed at its close. The House's 2025–26 committees each have two co-chairs; contact cards name the first listed.

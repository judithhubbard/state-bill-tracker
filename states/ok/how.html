The steps

  1. Introduction. Each Legislature lasts two years (the 60th is 2025–26). It meets each year from the first Monday in February until the last Friday in May at the latest. A bill has an author in each house: the principal author in the house where it's filed and a principal author in the other house. Bills that don't pass in the first year carry over to the second, with the same number, and die when the second session ends.
  2. Committee. In the House, a bill goes to an oversight committee and, within it, to a policy committee (Civil Judiciary under Judiciary and Public Safety Oversight, for example); the policy committee recommends it, then the oversight committee reports it. Bills that cost money go through Appropriations and Budget and its subcommittees the same way. Many House bills are first sent to the Rules Committee and later moved. In the Senate, a bill goes to a standing committee, and bills that cost money then to Appropriations. Most bills never get a hearing.
  3. Floor. A bill a committee approves goes on the General Order calendar, and each house's majority floor leader decides which ones come up. A bill passes on third reading with a majority of the house's members: 51 in the House, 25 in the Senate. An emergency clause, which lets a law take effect at once, needs two-thirds, voted on separately.
  4. The other house. The bill then goes through the other house's committees and floor. If the second house amends it, the first house has to agree and pass it again; if it won't, a conference committee writes a report both houses have to adopt.
  5. The governor. The Speaker and the President Pro Tempore sign the bill and it goes to the governor. While the Legislature meets, the governor has 5 days, Sundays excepted, to sign or veto it, and a bill not acted on becomes law. After the session ends the governor has 15 days, and a bill not signed by then dies (a pocket veto). The governor can veto items in spending bills. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house's members (68 representatives and 32 senators), or three-fourths (76 and 36) for a bill with an emergency clause. Laws are chapters of the year's session laws ("Chapter 1, O.S.L. 2025"); most take effect November 1.
  6. Constitutional amendments. The Legislature proposes an amendment to the Oklahoma Constitution with a joint resolution (HJR or SJR). It skips the governor: once it passes a majority of each house's members, it goes to the voters at the next general election, or at a special election the Legislature sets. Citizens can also put amendments on the ballot by petition.

How to have an impact

  • Get the bill a hearing. Most bills die in committee without a vote: committee chairs decide what's heard. Ask your legislators and the bill's authors to push for a hearing, and write the committee's members.
  • Watch the deadlines. Each session sets deadlines for committees to report bills and for each house to pass its own bills, in February, March and April. A bill that misses them usually waits for the next year, or dies.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to sign on as a coauthor and to vote for it.
  • Mind the end of the session. Many bills are rewritten in conference committees in May. Shell bills (placeholders with only a title) can become real bills late in the session.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the authors to take it up in the second year, or to file it again in the next Legislature.

About this data

Where the data comes from: LegiScan, which collects the Oklahoma Legislature's bills, histories, sponsors, roll-call votes and legislators and publishes them under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license; bill data on this site is from LegiScan, updated weekly, and more often while the Legislature meets. (The Legislature's own website asks automated readers not to use it.) Who holds each seat now, with party and email, comes from Open States' public-domain list of legislators; Capitol phone numbers and offices from LegiScan; cities, towns and counties by district from the Census Bureau's district relationship files.

House and Senate Bills of the regular sessions are shown, with the joint resolutions that propose constitutional amendments; other resolutions (including the joint resolutions that approve agencies' rules) and special sessions are left out. Topics come from the first words of each bill's official title (its subject) and the committees it went to, so a bill can have more than one. Shell bills, whose title only names a new act, are marked; statistics leave them out unless they moved past a committee.

There's no “What it does”: the data has only each bill's official title, and the bill texts and summaries are on the Legislature's website. Votes are shown member by member, in committee and on the floor. The data has no committee members or chairs, so contact cards don't name a committee's chair, and it has no chapter numbers for laws.

Stages are worked out from each bill's history. Governor's deadlines are estimates: 5 days, Sundays excepted, after the bill is sent, or 15 days after the session ends.

The steps

  1. Filing. Members file bills before the General Court opens in January of an odd year; citizens can file through their own legislator ("by request"). A General Court lasts two years, and bills stay alive until it ends.
  2. Joint committee. Each bill goes to a joint committee on its subject, with members of both houses. Every bill gets a public hearing, where anyone can testify. The committee reports it favorably, sends it to a "study order" (which ends it), or rewrites it as a new draft with a new number, often combining several bills.
  3. Ways and Means and scheduling. A bill the committee approves usually goes to House or Senate Ways and Means. In the House, Steering, Policy and Scheduling then decides when it reaches the floor.
  4. Floor. The bill gets three readings; on the third the house votes to pass it "to be engrossed". It then goes to the other house, which does the same. If the second house amends it, the first has to agree, or a conference committee writes a compromise.
  5. Enactment. Once both houses have passed the same text, the House and then the Senate vote to enact it, and it's laid before the governor.
  6. The governor. The governor has 10 days to sign it, veto it, or send it back with amendments, and can veto items in spending bills. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house.
  7. The calendar. Formal sessions end July 31 of the second year. After that, until the General Court ends in January, a bill passes only in an informal session, where any one member can stop it. Most laws take effect 90 days after they're signed.
  8. Constitutional amendments the Legislature proposes don't go to the governor. After a hearing in a joint committee, the House and Senate meet together in a joint session, the “constitutional convention.” An amendment approved there by a majority of all 200 members goes to the next General Court, which has to approve it again; then it goes to the voters at the next state election.

How to have an impact

  • Testify at the hearing. Every bill gets a public hearing in its joint committee. You can testify in person or online, or send written testimony to the committee, usually by email.
  • Ask the committee to report it. Most bills end in committee, sent to a study order. Write the House and Senate chairs, and ask your own legislators to talk to them.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor a bill and to vote for it.
  • Mind July 31. After formal sessions end in the second year, a bill can pass only if no member objects.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to file it again in the next General Court.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the General Court's open data service (each bill's history and sponsors, the Senate's roll calls, the members and committees), updated daily, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities and towns.

House and Senate bills and resolves are shown, and the Legislature's proposals for amendments to the Constitution, tagged “Constitutional amendments”; orders and other resolutions are left out. An amendment the joint session approves is shown as “Passed both houses.” A bill a committee rewrote under a new number is shown once, under its newest number, with the earlier numbers listed. Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and the words in its title, so a bill can have more than one.

The General Court doesn't publish summaries of bills in its data, so “What it does” isn't shown; the bill's page links to its text. Vote totals come from the bills' histories; the Senate's roll calls list how each senator voted. The House's roll calls aren't in the data.

Governor's deadlines are estimates from the Constitution's rule (10 days).

The steps

  1. Introduction. The Legislature meets once a year. In even years it's a general session: 60 legislative days within 85 calendar days, from the second Monday of March. In odd years it's a fiscal session: 45 legislative days within 60 calendar days, from the second Monday of April, for the budget and taxes; each member may introduce only five other bills. Members can prefile bills before the session. Bills that don't pass by the last day die.
  2. Committee. Each bill goes to a committee on its subject (prefiled bills are provisionally referred before the session). The committee hears it and reports it favorably, with amendments, or as a substitute (a new bill with its own number that takes the old one's place), or lets it sit; voting to defer it ends it for the session. Bills that spend money or change taxes often go on to a second committee: Appropriations or Ways and Means in the House, Finance or Revenue and Fiscal Affairs in the Senate.
  3. Floor. The Legislative Bureau reviews each reported bill's wording, then the full house debates it and votes on final passage by roll call. Passing a bill takes a majority of the house's members (53 representatives, 20 senators); some bills, such as constitutional amendments and new taxes, need two-thirds.
  4. The other house. The bill goes through committee and the floor again. If the second house changes it, the first house has to agree to the changes (concur); if it rejects them, a conference committee tries to settle the differences, and both houses have to adopt its report.
  5. The governor. The bill is enrolled, signed by the Speaker and the President of the Senate, and sent to the governor, who has 10 days to sign or veto it while the Legislature is in session, or 20 days after it's delivered when the session ends first; a bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law without a signature. The governor can veto items in an appropriation bill. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house's members (26 senators, 70 representatives), during the session or at a veto session held 40 days after it ends, unless a majority of either house's members say in writing it isn't needed. Laws are numbered Acts of the session (Act 28 of 2026); most take effect August 1.
  6. Constitutional amendments. Amendments to the Louisiana Constitution are bills (HB, SB) too. Two-thirds of each house's members (26 senators, 70 representatives) send one to the voters, at the statewide election it names; it never goes to the governor.

How to have an impact

  • Get the bill a hearing. Most bills that fail are never reported by their committee. Ask your legislators and the bill's author to press the committee chair, and write the committee members yourself.
  • Speak or fill out a card at the hearing. Committees take testimony on the bills they hear: anyone can fill out a witness card for or against a bill, and speak.
  • Get it to the floor. A reported bill still has to be called up for a vote. Ask your legislators to press for it.
  • Move quickly. The session lasts at most 85 calendar days in even years and 60 in odd years, and bills die when it ends.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask a legislator to introduce it again next session (in a fiscal year, as one of their five general bills).

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Legislature's website, legis.la.gov (each bill's page: title, authors, history, documents; the lists of vetoed bills and of constitutional amendments; the digests and the roll-call votes), the House's and the Senate's member and committee pages, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for places and parishes.

House and Senate bills of each regular session are shown, including the constitutional amendments; resolutions and special sessions are left out, as are bills withdrawn before they were introduced. Appropriation and capital outlay bills are marked "Budget bill". Topics come from the committees a bill went to and the subject that starts its title, so a bill can have more than one. Bills that needed a published notice because they affect one place (local and special bills) are marked as local.

"What it does" is the bill's digest, written by the Legislature's staff: the résumé digest of the act when it passed, else the digest of the newest version. Digests are still being downloaded, so some bills don't have one yet.

Floor votes on passing each bill (and on agreeing to the other house's changes and adopting conference reports) are shown member by member from the roll calls where they've been downloaded, else as the tally in the bill's history. From 2026, committee votes are shown member by member where the committee recorded one; otherwise the House committees' tallies. Votes are tied to today's legislators; members who have left appear by name.

Laws are bills with an Act number that the governor signed or let become law without a signature. They match the Legislature's bill statuses and its list of vetoed bills, bill for bill; Act numbers run from 1 with none missing (constitutional amendments get Act numbers too, but go to the voters).

Authors are matched to today's members by their district; authors who have left the Legislature are shown as the record names them.

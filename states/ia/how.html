The steps

  1. Introduction. The General Assembly sits for two years (2025–26 is the 91st), with a session each year from the second Monday of January to April or May. A bill not passed in the first year carries over to the second, where it left off; bills still not passed when the second session ends die. Many bills begin as study bills: drafts a committee takes up (often for the governor or a state agency) before deciding whether to introduce them.
  2. Committees. A bill goes to a committee, whose chair names a subcommittee of three members; the subcommittee holds a public meeting and recommends passage, amendments, or nothing. The committee then reports the bill, often by introducing it as its own bill under a new number ("renumbered as HF 581"). A bill can be renumbered more than once (by the policy committee, then by Appropriations); it's shown here as one bill under its newest number, with its earlier numbers. Committees don't vote bills down on the record: most bills that die are never reported.
  3. Deadlines. The "funnel" deadlines in the joint rules come about the sixth and tenth weeks of each session: by the first a bill has to be out of committee in its own house, by the second in the other house. Bills from the appropriations, ways and means and government oversight committees, and the leaders' bills, are exempt.
  4. Floor. Reported bills go on the calendar, and the majority leader of each house decides which come up for debate. A bill passes with a majority of the house's members (26 senators, 51 representatives), on a recorded vote.
  5. The other house. Many bills have a companion in the other house; when one passes, the other house usually takes it up in place of its own ("substituted for"), and its own is withdrawn. If the second house changes the bill, the first house agrees and passes it again, or a conference committee settles the differences.
  6. The governor. The governor has 3 days (Sundays excepted) to act on a bill sent during the session; for a bill sent in its last 3 days or after it (most are), 30 days after the session ends, and a bill not signed by then doesn't become law. The governor can veto items in an appropriation bill. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house's members (34 senators, 67 representatives). Laws are chapters of each year's Iowa Acts ("Chapter 46 of 2025"; the second year's are numbered from 1001).
  7. Constitutional amendments. Amendments to the Constitution of the State of Iowa are joint resolutions. One has to pass both houses, by a majority of each house's members, in two General Assemblies in a row, with the same text; then it goes to the voters at a general election. The governor has no part in it.

How to have an impact

  • Go to the subcommittee meeting. Every bill a committee takes up gets a public subcommittee meeting, usually announced a day or two ahead; anyone can speak or send comments.
  • Beat the funnel. A bill not out of committee by the funnel deadlines (February and March) is usually done for the year. Ask the committee chair, and your legislators, before then.
  • Follow the new numbers. A committee that approves a bill usually gives it a new number; it's shown here under the newest.
  • Follow both versions. Many bills have a companion in the other house; either can be the one that moves.
  • Bills that cost money. A bill that spends money has to get through Appropriations, and a tax bill through Ways and Means.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents.
  • Write the governor after the session. Most bills reach the governor in the last days of the session; the governor has 30 days to sign them.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please take it up"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Ask others to write too. Ten people writing once counts for more than one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.

About this data

Where the data comes from: each bill's history comes from legis.iowa.gov, or from Open States' copy of the same pages (public domain) where we don't have the official one yet. Both give the same history. Floor roll calls, member by member, are from Open States (about half of the passages; for the rest only the tally is shown); committee votes are tallies, without names. Legislators are from the House's and the Senate's member lists. Places are from the Census Bureau's district relationship files.

House and Senate Files of each two-year General Assembly are shown, and the joint resolutions proposing amendments to the state constitution; other joint resolutions (state symbols, nullifying agencies' rules, calls for a federal convention) and resolutions are left out. A bill a committee renumbered is one record under its newest number, with its earlier numbers and their histories; a study bill (a committee's draft) is part of the bill it became when its history has been read, and study bills a committee never introduced aren't shown. The sponsor is the member who filed the first number, or the committee. A bill that carried over from the first year to the second is one record with its whole history. Special sessions' bills (July 2023, October 2026) are numbered with the regular ones and shown with them. Topics come from the committees a bill went to, else words in its title.

Each bill's title is its official one ("A bill for an act relating to ..."), without the standard ending about effective-date provisions. The Legislative Services Agency's explanation of a bill, from its introduced text, is shown as "What it does" once it's been read; otherwise there's none.

Legislators' votes: names in the 2025–26 roll calls are tied to each member's seat; in 2023–24's, a surname is tied to a member of today's General Assembly in the same house when only one has it, and members who have left are shown by surname. The Capitol phone shown is each house's switchboard, which takes messages for every member. Committee memberships and chairs aren't shown yet.

Laws are bills the governor signed (some with items vetoed), each a chapter of the year's Iowa Acts. Counted by year: 2023, 167 laws (one, HF 732, from the July special session) and 1 veto (SF 388); 2024, 185 and 1 (HF 2539); 2025, 169 and 2 (HF 383, HF 639), matching the governor's actions as reported; 2026, 201 (one, HF 2801, from the October special session) and 5. Constitutional amendments that passed both houses: 2023–24, HJR 3 and SJR 9 (second passages, on the November 2024 ballot), HJR 2006 and SJR 2004 (first passages); 2025–26, SJR 9 and SJR 11.

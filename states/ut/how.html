The steps

  1. Introduction. A member, often on the recommendation of an interim committee, files a bill; the Office of Legislative Research and General Counsel drafts it. The Legislature meets each year for a 45-day general session from the third Tuesday of January to early March. Bills that don't pass by the end of the session die.
  2. Rules. A new bill goes first to its house's Rules Committee, which decides whether to send it to a standing committee for a hearing. Many bills wait there.
  3. Committee. The standing committee takes public comment and votes to recommend the bill (favorably, with amendments, or as a substitute), or holds it.
  4. Floor. A recommended bill goes on the calendar; the Rules Committee often decides which bills come up, especially late in the session. The House votes on third reading; the Senate on second and third reading. A bill needs a majority of all members: 38 in the House, 15 in the Senate.
  5. The other house. The bill goes to the other house and starts again in Rules. If the second house amends it, the first has to agree ("concur"), or a conference committee works out the differences.
  6. The governor. The governor has 10 days (Sundays excepted) during the session to sign or veto a bill, and 20 days after it adjourns. The governor can veto items in an appropriation bill. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house, and the Legislature can call itself back into session to do it.
  7. Constitutional amendments. The Legislature proposes an amendment to the Utah Constitution with a joint resolution (HJR or SJR). It needs two-thirds of each house's members and skips the governor: it goes to the voters at the next general election in November of an even year.
  8. The calendar. Most new laws take effect in early May, 60 days after the session ends; some take effect when signed or on a date the bill sets.

How to have an impact

  • Start before the session. Interim committees meet monthly between sessions, take public comment and recommend many of the next session's bills. That's often the best time to shape a bill.
  • Comment at the committee hearing. Standing committees take public comment on each bill, in person and usually online; the Legislature's website lists agendas a day or two ahead.
  • Move quickly. The session is only 45 days, and many bills never leave the Rules Committee.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor a bill and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsor or an interim committee to bring it back next session.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Legislature's developer data service (each bill's actions, sponsors, subjects, summaries and vote totals; the members and committees), how each member voted from the Legislature's vote pages, updated daily, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, towns and counties.

House and Senate bills of the general sessions are shown, with the joint resolutions that propose constitutional amendments; other resolutions and special sessions are left out. Topics come from the Legislature's subjects and the committees a bill was sent to, so a bill can have more than one.

“What it does” is the bill's General Description and Highlighted Provisions, written by the Office of Legislative Research and General Counsel. Votes list how each member voted, in committee and on the floor; members' votes are still being added for earlier sessions.

Governor's deadlines are estimates from the Constitution's rule (10 days during the session, Sundays excepted, and 20 days after it).

The steps

  1. Introduction. The Legislature meets in regular session only in odd-numbered years, for 120 days from the first Monday of February (the 83rd session was 2025; the 84th opens February 1, 2027). The Senate has 21 members and the Assembly 42. Bills are drafted by the Legislative Counsel Bureau; many are prefiled in the fall before the session, and many are filed by a committee on behalf of a state agency, a county, a city or another official, so their sponsor is a committee, not a member. Bills that don't pass by the end of the session die.
  2. Committee. Each bill goes to a standing committee of its house, which holds a hearing and, at a later "work session", may recommend "do pass" (often "amend, and do pass as amended"). Committees don't vote bills down: a bill a committee doesn't approve by the deadline dies there, as most do. Bills that cost money go on to Ways and Means (Assembly) or Finance (Senate).
  3. Deadlines. Joint Standing Rule 14.3 sets them: a bill has to pass a committee in its own house by day 68 and its own house by day 79, then the other house's committee by day 102 and the other house by day 109. Bills that spend money or affect revenue are exempt, and leaders can grant waivers.
  4. The floor. A bill reported by its committee is read a second time (amendments are adopted then) and a third time, when the house votes. Passing takes a majority of each house's members (22 in the Assembly, 11 in the Senate), or two-thirds for a bill that raises taxes. Every passage is a roll call.
  5. The other house. The bill goes through a committee and a floor vote again. If the second house amends it, the first has to agree ("concur"); if it doesn't, a conference committee writes a compromise that both houses have to adopt.
  6. The governor. Bills that pass both houses are enrolled and delivered to the governor, who has 5 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto them during the session, or 10 days after it adjourns. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. Laws are numbered as chapters of the Statutes of Nevada, from 1 each session; most take effect October 1.
  7. Vetoes. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house: 28 Assembly members and 14 senators. A bill vetoed during the session goes back to its house at once; one vetoed after the session goes to the next regular session, two years later, which can override it then. Governor Joe Lombardo vetoed 75 bills in 2023, then a record for Nevada, and 87 in 2025; the 2025 session overrode none of the 2023 vetoes.
  8. Constitutional amendments. A joint resolution proposing an amendment has to pass both houses, by a majority of each house's members, in two sessions in a row, without changes; then it goes to the voters at the next general election. Amendments never go to the governor. (Voters can also amend the constitution by initiative petition, which doesn't go through the Legislature.)
  9. The Lieutenant Governor. The Lieutenant Governor presides over the Senate and isn't a senator; the Majority Leader runs it. The Speaker leads the Assembly.

How to have an impact

  • Speak at the hearing. Committees take public comment on the bills they hear, in Carson City, by video from the Grant Sawyer State Office Building in Las Vegas, or by phone. You can also send a written comment or register your position on a bill in NELIS before the hearing.
  • Mind the deadlines. Most bills die at the committee deadline (day 68) or the house deadline (day 79). A word to the committee chair before then counts most.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state senator and one Assembly member, and they listen most to their own constituents.
  • Remember the odd years. The Legislature meets only from February to June of odd-numbered years. Between sessions, ask a legislator to request a bill draft for the next one.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the Assembly; please support it in the Senate"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.

About this data

Where the data comes from: NELIS, the Nevada Legislature's information system at leg.state.nv.us (the bill lists, each bill's overview with its sponsors, title, Legislative Counsel's Digest, hearings and history, the final passage votes with how each member voted, the lists of bills that became law and were vetoed, the members and committees); members' email addresses from Open States' public-domain file (data.openstates.org), since the Legislature's pages hide them from programs; and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, towns, communities and counties.

Assembly and Senate bills of each regular session are shown, with the joint resolutions that propose amendments to the Nevada Constitution; other resolutions and special sessions are left out. A resolution passed a second time in a later session is listed there under its first session's number, and isn't in the records read here. Each bill's title is its official one-line summary; its summary is the full title ("AN ACT relating to ..."). Topics come from the part of the Nevada Revised Statutes the bill draft amends, the committees a bill was sent to and words in its title, so a bill can have more than one.

"What it does" is the Legislative Counsel's Digest, the plain account printed at the start of each bill. It's shown with what the bill does first and the existing law it changes after, without the statute citations; it describes the latest version of the bill.

Many bills are sponsored by a committee, on behalf of an agency, a local government or an official (more than half in 2023, about 40% in 2025). They're shown with the committee as sponsor, and legislators' sponsorship numbers count only the bills they sponsored themselves.

Committees' recommendations come from the history and the hearings; the committees' own votes are only in their minutes. Floor votes are the final passage roll calls, member by member.

A bill vetoed after the session goes back to the next regular session, so the veto isn't final until that session ends.

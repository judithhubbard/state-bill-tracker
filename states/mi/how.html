The steps

  1. Introduction. A senator or representative introduces a bill, often with others as a package of "tie-barred" bills that only take effect together. The Legislature meets all year; a session lasts two years, and bills stay alive until it ends December 31 of the second year.
  2. Committee. The Speaker or the Senate Majority Leader sends the bill to a standing committee. Its chair decides whether it gets a hearing and a vote.
  3. Floor. In the House, a reported bill gets a second reading (where amendments are made) and a third reading, the vote. In the Senate, the Committee of the Whole takes up amendments first. A bill needs a majority of all members: 56 in the House, 20 in the Senate.
  4. The other house. The bill starts again in a committee of the other house. If that house changes it, the first has to concur, or a conference committee works out the differences. Then the bill is enrolled.
  5. The governor. The governor has 14 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto it, and can veto items in a spending bill. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law, unless the session has ended. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house.
  6. Constitutional amendments. An amendment to the state constitution is a joint resolution, lettered rather than numbered ("HJR A"). It needs two-thirds of the members of each house (74 in the House, 26 in the Senate), doesn't go to the governor, and goes to the voters at the next general election, in November of an even year, unless the Legislature calls a special election.
  7. When it takes effect. A new law takes effect 91 days after the end of that year's session, unless two-thirds of each house vote to give it "immediate effect".

How to have an impact

  • Ask the committee chair. A bill moves only if the chair gives it a hearing and a vote. Write the chair and members, and ask your own legislators to talk to them.
  • Testify or send a card. At a committee hearing you can testify, or fill out a card saying you support or oppose the bill.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor a bill and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to introduce it again in the next session.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Legislature's bill status files (each bill's sponsors, subjects and history) and its web service (legislators' emails and phones), updated daily, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities and townships. Party comes from the caucus websites the Legislature lists for each member.

House and Senate bills are shown, with the joint resolutions proposing amendments to the state constitution; other resolutions are left out. A bill's title is its digest title. Topics come from the Legislature's subject categories and the words in the title, so a bill can have more than one.

The status files have no summaries, so “What it does” isn't shown; each bill's page links to its text and to the House and Senate Fiscal Agencies' analyses. Floor votes show the totals; how each member voted is in the journals.

Governor's deadlines are estimates from the Constitution's rule (14 days, Sundays excepted).

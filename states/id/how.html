The steps

  1. Introduction. The Legislature meets once a year, from the second Monday of January until it adjourns, usually in late March or April. Most bills are introduced by a committee: a member (or a state agency, or a group) presents an idea at a "print hearing", and if the committee votes to print it, the bill carries the committee's name. The member who asked is named in the bill's Statement of Purpose. Members can also introduce bills personally, but those are filed with the clerk and rarely go further. Bills that don't pass by the end of the session die.
  2. Committee. The printed bill goes to a standing committee on its subject, which holds a public hearing and votes: "do pass", or send it to the amending stage, or hold it. A bill the committee never reports out dies there. The budget bills, written by the Joint Finance-Appropriations Committee, skip this step.
  3. Floor. A bill that needs changes goes to the whole house for amendment first (General Orders in the House, the 14th Order in the Senate). Then it's read a second and third time and voted on. Each vote is recorded member by member; a bill voted down is dead unless the house reconsiders the vote.
  4. The other house. The bill goes through committee and the floor again. If the second house amends it, the first house has to agree ("concur") and pass it again before it can go on.
  5. The governor. The bill is enrolled, signed by the Speaker and the President of the Senate, and delivered to the governor, who has 5 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto it while the Legislature is in session, or 10 days after it adjourns. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. The governor can veto items in an appropriation bill. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of the members present in each house. Laws are chapters of that year's Session Laws.
  6. Constitutional amendments. The Legislature proposes an amendment to the Idaho Constitution with a joint resolution (HJR or SJR). It skips the governor: two-thirds of each house's members send it to the voters at the next general election.
  7. The calendar. Most laws take effect on July 1, unless the bill has an emergency clause or sets another date.

How to have an impact

  • Get the bill a hearing. Most bills that fail are never taken up by their committee. Ask your legislators and the members carrying the bill to press the chair, and write the committee members yourself.
  • Testify. Committees take public testimony in person at the Capitol and remotely; sign up on the committee's page before the hearing.
  • Move quickly. Sessions are short, and a bill can go from introduction to the governor in a few weeks.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state senator and two state representatives, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask a legislator to bring it back next session; interim committees study issues between sessions.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Idaho Legislature's website (legislature.idaho.gov): each year's list of bills with its tables of enacted and vetoed legislation, each bill's page (sponsor, description and history, with each floor vote member by member), each bill's Statement of Purpose, each year's committee pages and the current member lists; and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities and counties.

House and Senate bills are shown, with the joint resolutions that propose constitutional amendments; other resolutions, memorials and extraordinary sessions are left out. Appropriation bills are marked "Budget bill". Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and words in its subject and title, so a bill can have more than one.

Most bills are introduced by a committee, so the sponsor shown is the committee; the member who asked for the bill is named in its Statement of Purpose (linked from the bill's page), and the members who carried it on the floor are in its history. Legislators' sponsorship counts include only the few bills they introduced personally.

Each bill's title is the Legislature's short title and its description is a one-line note of what it changes. "What it does" is the bill's Statement of Purpose, written for its sponsor (so it can argue for the bill), without the fiscal note and contact; the sponsor can revise it, and the newest copy is shown. Committee votes aren't published (they're in the minutes). Votes by members who have left the Legislature are shown by name only.

The steps

  1. Introduction. The Legislature meets once a year for 30 legislative days within 105 calendar days: from the first Tuesday of February, or the second Tuesday of January in an election year, or the first Tuesday of March in the year after one. Members can prefile bills before it opens. A bill is read the first time and sent to a committee. Bills that don't pass by the end of the session die.
  2. Committee. A standing committee on the bill's subject holds a public hearing and votes. Alabama's committees report a bill favorably (often with amendments or a substitute) or not at all; a bill the committee doesn't take up dies there. Local bills, for one county or city, go to the Local Legislation Committee or to the county's own delegation committee.
  3. Floor. A reported bill is read a second time and placed on the calendar. Each house's Rules Committee sets the special order calendars that decide which bills come up. Until the two budgets pass, a bill first needs a "budget isolation resolution" (three-fifths of those voting) to be taken up ahead of them. Then it's read a third time and voted on, member by member. A bill can also be "indefinitely postponed", often because its companion bill in the other house passed instead.
  4. The other house. The bill goes through committee and the floor again. If the second house amends it, the first house has to agree ("concur"), or the two houses name a conference committee, whose report both have to adopt.
  5. The governor. The bill is enrolled, signed by the presiding officers and delivered to the governor, who has 6 days (Sundays excepted) to sign it, veto it or send it back with a proposed amendment (an "executive amendment"); a bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. After the session ends, a bill not signed within 10 days dies: a pocket veto. Overriding a veto takes a majority of each house's members (18 senators, 53 representatives). Laws are numbered Acts of each year (Act 2025-312).
  6. Constitutional amendments. Amendments to the Constitution of Alabama are bills too, marked "Constitutional Amendment". Three-fifths of each house's members send one to the voters, never to the governor: statewide, or for a local amendment only in the county it concerns.
  7. Local bills. About a fifth of the bills apply to one county or city. A local bill's notice has to be published in the county before it's introduced ("N & P" on the bill: notice and proof). They're marked "For one city or county" here.

How to have an impact

  • Get the bill a hearing. Most bills that fail are never taken up by their committee. Ask your legislators and the bill's sponsor to press the chair, and write the committee members yourself.
  • Testify. Committees hold public hearings at the State House in Montgomery; check the committee's meeting notice for how to sign up to speak.
  • Get it on the calendar. A reported bill still needs the Rules Committee to put it on a special order calendar. Ask your legislators to press for it.
  • Move quickly. The session is 30 legislative days, and a bill can go from introduction to the governor in a few weeks.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state senator and one state representative, and they listen most to their own constituents. For a local bill, your county's delegation decides.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask a legislator to prefile it for next session.

About this data

Where the data comes from: ALISON, the Alabama Legislature's website: each year's bills (sponsors, subject, committees, status, Act numbers and the acts' summaries), every bill's history, each roll call member by member, each bill's text (for the synopsis and the official title), and the members and committees; and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, towns and counties.

House and Senate bills of each regular session are shown, including the constitutional amendments; resolutions and special sessions are left out. Appropriation bills are marked "Budget bill", and local bills "For one city or county". Topics come from the Legislature's subject for each bill and the committees it went to, so a bill can have more than one.

"What it does" is the act's summary for a law, and otherwise the Legislative Services Agency's synopsis of the bill as introduced (it may have changed since). Bills first filed as amended versions, and most local bills, have no synopsis.

Votes shown are the floor votes on passing a bill, agreeing to the other house's amendments and adopting a conference report. The votes on budget isolation resolutions and amendments are listed in a bill's history but not counted as votes on the bill. Members who abstained or passed are shown as "Abstained". Most members abstain on local bills, which only the county's delegation votes on. Committee votes aren't published.

The governor's actions come from the bills' histories: "Enacted" with an Act number is a law. A bill still on the governor's desk 10 days after the session ended wasn't signed, so it died (a pocket veto); one the governor sent back with an amendment the Legislature didn't take up died too. Both are counted as vetoes here.

The steps

  1. Introduction. A member, or a committee, introduces a bill (many are filed before the session). The Legislature meets each year: a long session in odd years (January to late June, 160 days at most) and a short one in even years (February to March, 35 days). Bills that don't pass by the end of the session die.
  2. Committee. The Speaker or the Senate President sends the bill to a committee on its subject. The committee holds a public hearing, where anyone can testify, then votes in a work session. Bills that cost money go on to the Joint Committee on Ways and Means.
  3. Floor. A bill the committee recommends gets a second and third reading; the full house votes on third reading. A bill needs a majority of all members: 31 in the House, 16 in the Senate (three-fifths for a bill that raises revenue).
  4. The other house. The bill goes to the other house and starts again in committee. If the second house amends it, the first has to agree ("concur") or a conference committee works out the differences.
  5. The governor. During the session, the governor has 5 days (Saturdays and Sundays excepted) to sign or veto a bill; bills that arrive at the end get 30 business days after adjournment. The governor can veto items in a budget bill. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house.
  6. Constitutional amendments. The Legislature proposes an amendment to the Oregon Constitution with a joint resolution (HJR or SJR). It goes through committees and both floors like a bill, but needs a majority of each house's members (a full revision needs two-thirds), and it skips the governor: it goes to the voters at the next general election in November of an even year, unless the Legislature calls a special election. Citizens can also put amendments on the ballot by initiative petition.
  7. The calendar. Many new laws take effect January 1 of the next year; others on the 91st day after the session ends, or sooner with an emergency clause.

How to have an impact

  • Testify at the public hearing. Anyone can register to testify in person or remotely, or send written testimony, through the Legislature's website once a hearing is scheduled.
  • Mind the deadlines. Committees must act on bills by set dates, and the sessions are short, especially in even years. A bill that misses a deadline usually dies.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor a bill and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to introduce it again next session.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Legislature's open data service (each bill's history, sponsors, digest and votes; the members and committees), updated daily, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities and counties.

House and Senate bills of the regular sessions are shown, with the joint resolutions that propose constitutional amendments; other resolutions, memorials and special sessions are left out. Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and the words in its title and digest, so a bill can have more than one.

“What it does” is the bill's digest, a plain-language summary written by the Legislature's staff. Votes list how each member voted, in committee and on the floor.

Governor's deadlines are estimates from the Constitution's rule (5 weekdays during the session, 30 business days after it).

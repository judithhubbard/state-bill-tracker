The steps

  1. Introduction. The General Assembly meets once a year from early January: 60 legislative days in even years (ending by April 15, with the two-year budget) and 30 in odd years (a few days in January, then February and March, ending by March 30). Members can prefile bills before it opens. Bills that don't pass by the end of the session die.
  2. Committee on Committees. Each house's Committee on Committees, made up of its leaders, assigns every bill to a standing committee. Many bills are never assigned, and die there.
  3. Committee. A standing committee on the bill's subject hears it and reports it favorably, often with a committee substitute or amendments, or doesn't take it up at all; a bill the committee doesn't report dies there. A bill is read the first and second time while it's in committee.
  4. Floor. A reported bill goes to the Rules Committee (the house's leaders again), which posts bills for a vote in the orders of the day; uncontroversial ones can go on a consent calendar. Then it's read a third time and voted on.
  5. The other house. The bill goes through the Committee on Committees, a committee and the floor again. If the second house changes it, the first house has to agree ("concur") and pass it again, or the second house can drop its changes ("recede"); otherwise a conference committee, and then a free conference committee, try to settle the differences, and both houses have to adopt the result.
  6. The governor. The bill is enrolled, signed by the Speaker and the President of the Senate, and delivered to the governor, who has 10 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto it; a bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law without a signature. The governor can veto items in an appropriation bill. Overriding a veto takes a majority of each house's members (20 senators, 51 representatives), and the General Assembly breaks for about ten days before its last two so it can take up vetoes. A veto after the last day stands. Laws are numbered chapters of each year's Acts (Acts ch. 4); most take effect 90 days after the session ends.
  7. Constitutional amendments. Amendments to the Constitution of Kentucky are bills too ("AN ACT proposing to amend Section ..."). Three-fifths of each house's members (23 senators, 60 representatives) send one to the voters at the next November election for the House, never to the governor. No more than four go on one ballot.

How to have an impact

  • Get the bill assigned and heard. Most bills that fail are never assigned to a committee, or never taken up by it. Ask your legislators and the bill's sponsor to press the leaders and the committee chair, and write the committee members yourself.
  • Testify. Committees meet in the Capitol Annex in Frankfort; the Legislature's calendar lists each meeting, and chairs decide who speaks. Written testimony can go to the committee's staff.
  • Get it posted. A reported bill still needs the Rules Committee to post it for a vote. Ask your legislators to press for it.
  • Move quickly. The sessions are short (30 legislative days in odd years), and a bill can go from introduction to the governor in a few weeks.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state senator and one state representative, and they listen most to their own constituents. You can also leave a message for any legislator on the LRC's message line, (502) 564-8100.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask a legislator to prefile it for next session.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Legislative Research Commission's bill record for each regular session (each bill's title, sponsors, the LRC's summaries, index headings and actions, and its lists of laws and vetoes), the General Assembly's member and committee pages, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities and counties.

House and Senate bills of each regular session are shown, including the constitutional amendments; joint resolutions (which also go to the governor, mostly road namings and directions to state agencies), other resolutions, special sessions and bills withdrawn before they were introduced are left out. Appropriation bills are marked "Budget bill". Topics come from the committees a bill went to and the LRC's index headings, so a bill can have more than one.

"What it does" is the LRC's summary of the enacted version for a law, and otherwise its summary of the bill as introduced (it may have changed since).

Votes shown are the floor tallies from each bill's history. How each member voted is published only in PDFs (each bill's vote history, linked from the bill), which aren't read here, so members' votes are missing. Committee votes aren't shown.

Laws are bills with a chapter in that year's Acts: signed by the governor, law without the governor's signature, or law over a veto. They match the LRC's lists of laws and vetoes bill for bill.

Sponsors are matched to today's members by name; sponsors who have left the General Assembly are shown as the record names them ("D. Yates"). Legislators' phone numbers are mostly the LRC's message line, (502) 564-8100, which takes messages for every member.

The steps

  1. Committee. Each bill is introduced in the Senate or the Assembly and sent to a committee on its subject. Bills that cost money often go to a second committee: Assembly Appropriations or Senate Budget and Appropriations. The committee chair decides which bills get a hearing, and most bills never get one.
  2. Floor. When a committee approves a bill (“reports” it), the bill is on “second reading,” waiting for a vote of the full house. The Senate President and the Assembly Speaker decide which bills come up for a vote.
  3. The other house. A bill that passes one house goes to the other. Most bills have an identical bill in the other house. When the passed bill arrives, the other house usually swaps it in for its own version (“substitutes” it) and votes on it. Both houses have to pass the same text.
  4. On the governor's desk. A bill that passes both houses goes to the governor, who has 45 days to sign it, veto it, or send it back with changes (a “conditional veto”). If both houses vote to accept the changes, the bill goes back to the governor to sign. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house: 27 senators and 54 Assembly members.
  5. The two-year session. A session starts on the second Tuesday of January in an even year and runs two years. Bills carry over from the first year to the second. When the session ends, every bill that hasn't become law dies and has to be reintroduced with a new number. Bills passed in the last days go to the governor, who has until a week after the session ends to sign them; a bill left unsigned dies (a “pocket veto”).
  6. Constitutional amendments are concurrent resolutions (ACR, SCR), and they don't go to the governor. After a public hearing, one that passes both houses by three-fifths of all members (24 senators and 48 Assembly members) goes to the voters at the next general election. One that passes by a smaller majority has to pass both houses again the next year before it goes to the voters.

How to have an impact

  • Contact whoever decides the next step. In committee, that's the committee chair. On the floor, it's the Senate President or the Assembly Speaker, who choose which bills get a vote. Once a bill has passed both houses, it's the governor's office.
  • Bring in your own legislators. Each district has one senator and two Assembly members, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor a bill, to press the chair or leaders to move it, and to vote for it.
  • Time it. Committee agendas usually come out a few days before a meeting, so write to the chair before then. A bill sent to the governor starts a 45-day clock; if you write the governor only once, write then.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed both houses; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to reintroduce it next session.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the New Jersey Legislature's bill database (every bill, its actions, sponsors, subjects and committees) and its vote files (floor and committee votes).

Identical bills in the two houses are shown together, and the stage is the furthest either one reached. A bill reintroduced in the next session gets a new number; the history under each bill follows the Legislature's links to the last session.

Topics come from the Legislature's own subject list and the committees a bill was sent to, so a bill can have more than one. Concurrent resolutions proposing amendments to the State Constitution are included, tagged “Constitutional amendments”; other resolutions are left out. The data doesn't mark which resolutions propose amendments, so they're picked by their synopsis and subjects. Party is from each session's list of legislators.

Governor's deadlines are estimates: the records don't say when a bill reached the governor, so the 45 days are counted from the day it passed both houses.

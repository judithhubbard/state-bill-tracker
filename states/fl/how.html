The steps

  1. Filing. The Legislature meets once a year for 60 days: from early March in odd years, and from January in even years. Members file bills in the months before (the next session's appear from the summer), and most bills are filed in both houses, as companions with the same or similar text. Bills that don't pass by the last day die.
  2. Committees. The Senate President or the Speaker refers each bill to its committees, usually two to four in a row; in the House, a subcommittee first, then a full committee. A committee can approve it, amend it, or rewrite it as a committee substitute ("CS/HB 1195"), which keeps its number. Bills that spend money also go to an appropriations or budget committee. Most bills are never heard, and die in committee.
  3. Floor. A bill through its committees goes on the calendar; the Senate Rules Committee and the House's leaders choose which bills are put on the special order calendar for a vote. It's read a second time (when amendments are taken up) and a third time, then passed by roll call: a majority of those voting, two-thirds for some bills (a new public records exemption, for one).
  4. The other house. The bill goes to the other house, which usually takes it up in place of its own companion ("substituted"). If the second house changes it, the first house has to agree to the changes and pass it again, or the houses work out their differences, sometimes in a conference committee. A bill both houses passed in the same text is ordered enrolled.
  5. The governor. The Senate President and the Speaker sign the enrolled bill and present it to the governor when they choose, often weeks or months after the session. The governor then has 7 days to sign or veto it, or 15 days if the session has ended; a bill not acted on becomes law without a signature. The governor can veto items in an appropriation bill. A veto goes to the next regular session, where two-thirds of each house can override it. Laws are chapters of the Laws of Florida ("Chapter 2025-19"); most take effect July 1 or October 1.
  6. Constitutional amendments. Amendments to the State Constitution are joint resolutions (HJR, SJR). Three-fifths of each house's members (24 senators, 72 representatives) put one on the ballot at the next general election, where it needs 60% of the vote; it never goes to the governor.

How to have an impact

  • Get the bill a hearing. Most bills that fail are never taken up by one of their committees. Ask your legislators and the bill's sponsor to press the committee chair, and write the committee members yourself.
  • Follow both versions. Most bills have a companion in the other house; the one that moves first usually carries it. Watch both.
  • Speak at the hearing. Committees take public testimony: fill out an appearance card at the meeting to speak for or against a bill, or to waive your time in support.
  • Get it on the calendar. A bill through its committees still has to be scheduled for a vote by the house's leaders. Ask your legislators to press for it.
  • Move quickly. The session lasts 60 days, and bills die when it ends.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask a legislator to file it again next session.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Florida Senate's website, flsenate.gov (each bill's page, which covers House bills too: title, sponsors, history, votes, staff analyses), the House's and the Senate's member and committee pages, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for places and counties. Bills not yet read from the Senate's site come from Open States (public domain), which copies the same pages: the same history, with roll calls member by member.

House and Senate bills of each regular session are shown, with the joint resolutions that propose constitutional amendments; resolutions, memorials and special sessions are left out, as are bills withdrawn before they were introduced. Appropriation and budget bills are marked "Budget bill"; local bills (for one city or county) and claim bills ("Relief of ...", payments to one person) are marked as narrow. Topics come from the committees a bill went to, else words in its title and description. Each house's bill is its own record, even when its companion passed instead (it then shows as replaced).

"What it does" is the summary of the bill's newest staff analysis, written by the nonpartisan committee staff, where it's been downloaded. Every bill also shows its one-line description from the Senate's site under its title. Analyses are still being downloaded, so many bills have only the description for now.

Floor votes on each bill's passage are shown member by member, from the vote records where they've been downloaded, else from Open States' copies of them. Committee votes are shown member by member for Senate committees (and House committees from 2026), else as the tally in the Senate's history; House committees' tallies aren't in the histories.

Laws are bills the governor signed or let become law without a signature (each gets a chapter number in the Laws of Florida). They match the last actions on the Senate's bill lists, bill for bill.

Neither house publishes legislators' email addresses: each legislator's contact form is linked instead.

The steps

  1. Filing. A member of the House or Senate files a bill. Bills for a session can be filed from the Monday after the November election before it; most have to be filed by the session's 60th day, in mid-March. The Legislature meets in regular session only in odd years, for 140 days from the second Tuesday of January (the 2025 session was the 89th Legislature).
  2. Committee. The Speaker (in the House) or the Lieutenant Governor (in the Senate) sends the bill to a committee. The committee holds a public hearing, where anyone can testify, and votes. A bill that's heard but not voted on is “left pending,” and most never come back.
  3. Calendars (House). A House bill a committee approves goes to the Calendars Committee, which decides whether and when it reaches the floor. Many bills die there in the session's last weeks.
  4. Floor. The full house votes, on second and third reading. In the Senate, the Lieutenant Governor decides which bills come up, and bringing one up takes the votes of 18 of the 31 senators. The House has to pass its own bills by the session's 122nd day, in mid-May.
  5. The other house. The bill goes to the other house under the same number, where a member “sponsors” it, and starts again in a committee. Many bills have an identical “companion” filed in the other house, listed under “Similar bills.” If the second house changes the bill, the first has to agree, or a conference committee works out one text.
  6. The governor. A bill that passes both houses goes to the governor, who has 10 days (not counting Sundays) to sign or veto it during the session, or until 20 days after the session ends for bills passed in its last days. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. A veto after the session ends can't be overridden, so most vetoes are final. Most new laws take effect September 1.
  7. Special sessions. The governor can call the Legislature back for special sessions of up to 30 days, on topics the governor chooses. Their bills are numbered separately and aren't shown here.

How to have an impact

  • Act early. The session is short and comes only every other year. Ask your legislators to file a bill before the session, from the November before; most bills have to be filed by mid-March.
  • Testify at the hearing. Committees take public testimony in person, and House committees also take written comments online. Hearings are posted at least a few days ahead, so watch for your bill.
  • Contact whoever decides the next step. In committee, that's the chair and members. For a House bill out of committee, it's the Calendars Committee. Once it has passed both houses, it's the governor.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-author a bill and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the authors to file it again for the next session.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Texas Legislature's public data files (each bill's history, authors, sponsors, subjects and committee votes), the House and Senate websites for legislators and committees, the Legislative Reference Library for parties, and the Texas Legislative Council's district reports for counties.

House and Senate bills from regular sessions are shown; resolutions and special sessions' bills are left out. Each bill is shown under its own number; its companion in the other house, if it has one, is listed under “Similar bills.” Topics come from the Legislature's subject index and the words in a bill's caption, so a bill can have more than one.

Committee votes are totals from the bill histories; floor votes, with how each member voted, come from the House and Senate journals (final passage, agreeing to the other house's changes, and conference reports).

Governor's deadlines are estimates: 10 days (not counting Sundays) after a bill reached the governor, or 20 days after the session ended.

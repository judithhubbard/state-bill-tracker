The steps

  1. Introduction. A legislator, often with co-sponsors gathered by a memo, introduces a bill drafted by the Legislative Reference Bureau. A session lasts two years; the Legislature meets in floor periods, the last for general business in the spring of the second year. Bills still pending then fail.
  2. Committee. The bill goes to a standing committee. Its chair decides whether it gets a public hearing and an executive session, where the committee votes.
  3. Scheduling. A bill the committee recommends goes to the Assembly Committee on Rules or the Committee on Senate Organization, which decide which bills reach the floor. Bills that cost money may go to the Joint Committee on Finance first.
  4. Floor. The bill is read a second time, when amendments are taken up, and a third time, when the house votes.
  5. The other house. The bill starts again in a committee of the other house. If that house amends it, the first has to concur in the amendment.
  6. Constitutional amendments. An amendment to the state constitution is a joint resolution ("AJR 5"). It must pass both houses, by a majority of their members, in two Legislatures in a row: on "first consideration", and again, in the same words, on "second consideration" after an election. It doesn't go to the governor; after the second passage it goes to the voters, at an election the resolution sets (often the April spring election).
  7. The governor. The governor has 6 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto it, and can veto parts of a spending bill. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house.

How to have an impact

  • Testify at the public hearing. Anyone can testify, or register for or against a bill, at a committee's public hearing.
  • Ask the committee chair. A bill moves only if the chair schedules a hearing and a vote. Write the chair and members, and ask your own legislators to talk to them.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor a bill and to vote for it.
  • Mind the floor periods. Each house votes only in scheduled floor periods; the last for general business is in the spring of the second year.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the Assembly; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Legislature's documents site (each bill's history, authors, the Legislative Reference Bureau's analysis and the roll calls; the members and committees), updated daily, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, villages and towns.

Assembly and Senate bills of the regular session are shown, with the joint resolutions proposing amendments to the state constitution; other joint resolutions, other resolutions and special sessions are left out. Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and the words in its title, so a bill can have more than one.

“What it does” is the Legislative Reference Bureau's analysis of the bill as introduced. Floor votes list how each member voted.

Governor's deadlines are estimates from the Constitution's rule (6 days, Sundays excepted).

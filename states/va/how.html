The steps

  1. Committee. Each bill is introduced in the House of Delegates or the Senate and sent to a committee on its subject. In the House, a subcommittee usually hears it first. Bills that cost money also go to House Appropriations or Senate Finance and Appropriations. Committees decide the fate of most bills: they report a bill, kill it by voting it down or by “passing it by indefinitely,” or, in an even-year session, “continue” it to the next year.
  2. Floor. A bill a committee reports goes to the floor of its house, where it is read three times and voted on. Most reported bills get a vote.
  3. Crossover. Partway through the session, each house has to finish its own bills. Bills that haven't passed their own house by “crossover” day are dead for the year.
  4. The other house. A bill that passes one house goes to the other under the same number and starts again in a committee. Many bills have an identical companion bill in the other house. Both houses have to pass the same text; if the second house changes it, the first has to agree.
  5. The governor. A bill that passes both houses goes to the governor, who can sign it, veto it, or send it back with amendments. The governor has 7 days while the session is running; for bills that arrive in its last week or after, 30 days after it adjourns.
  6. The reconvened session. About six weeks after adjourning, the Legislature meets for a day to vote on the governor's amendments and vetoes. If both houses accept the amendments, the bill becomes law with them. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of the members present in each house.
  7. The year's session. A session starts on the second Wednesday of January and runs 60 days in even years and 46 in odd ones. Bills that don't pass die when it ends, except those continued from an even-year session to the next year. New laws usually take effect on July 1.

How to have an impact

  • Act early. The session is short, and most decisions come in its first weeks, in committees and subcommittees. A bill that misses crossover is dead for the year.
  • Contact whoever decides the next step. In committee, that's the committee or subcommittee chair and its members. Once a bill has passed both houses, it's the governor's office.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state senator and one delegate, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor a bill and to vote for it.
  • Time it. Committee dockets come out a few days before a meeting, so write before then. Once a bill reaches the governor, there are only 7 days during the session.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed both houses; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to introduce it again next year.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Legislative Information System's data files (every bill, its history, patrons, votes, summaries and committee dockets), and the House and Senate websites for legislators and committees.

Each bill is shown under its own number; its companion in the other house, if it has one, is listed under “Similar bills.” Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and the words in its title, so a bill can have more than one. Resolutions are left out.

“What it does” is the Division of Legislative Services' summary of the latest version of the bill.

Governor's deadlines are estimates: 7 days after a bill reached the governor, or 30 days after the session adjourned.

The steps

  1. Introduction. A member files a bill, drafted by the Legislative Services Agency. The General Assembly meets each year: a long session in odd years, when it writes the two-year budget, which must end by April 29, and a short session in even years, which must end by March 14. Bills that don't pass by the end of the session die.
  2. Committee. On first reading the Speaker or the Senate President Pro Tempore sends the bill to a standing committee. The committee chair decides whether it gets a hearing; a bill that isn't heard dies there. After a hearing the committee votes to recommend it ("do pass", often with amendments), and the house adopts the committee's report. House bills that cost money go on to Ways and Means; Senate bills can be sent on to Appropriations or Tax and Fiscal Policy.
  3. Floor. On second reading any member can offer amendments; on third reading the house votes on the bill. It needs a majority of all members: 51 in the House, 26 in the Senate. Each house sets a deadline, about halfway through the session, for passing its own bills.
  4. The other house. The bill goes through committee and the floor again. If the second house amends it, the first house either agrees ("concurs") or dissents, and a conference committee writes a report that both houses have to adopt.
  5. The governor. The Speaker, the President Pro Tempore and the Lieutenant Governor sign the bill, and it's presented to the governor, who has 7 days to sign or veto it. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. There's no line-item veto. Overriding a veto takes a majority of each house's members, and the General Assembly can do it until the end of its next regular session. Laws are numbered as Public Laws (P.L. 126-2025).
  6. Constitutional amendments. The General Assembly proposes an amendment to the Indiana Constitution with a joint resolution (HJR or SJR). It skips the governor: a majority of each house's members has to agree in two General Assemblies in a row, with an election between, and then it goes to the voters at the next general election.
  7. The calendar. Most new laws take effect July 1; some take effect when signed or on a date the bill sets.

How to have an impact

  • Get the bill a hearing. In Indiana most bills die because the committee chair never schedules them. Ask your legislators and the bill's authors to press the chair, and write the chair yourself.
  • Testify at the committee hearing. Committees take public testimony, in person at the Statehouse; meetings are streamed online and agendas are posted a few days ahead.
  • Move quickly. Sessions are short, and each house's deadline for passing its own bills comes about halfway through.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to sign on as a coauthor or sponsor and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the author or an interim study committee to take it up before next session.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Indiana General Assembly's data service (each bill's actions, authors and sponsors, versions with their digests, subjects and vote totals; the members and committees), updated daily; legislators' emails and links to their caucus pages from Open States' public-domain list of legislators; and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, towns and counties.

House and Senate bills of the regular sessions are shown, with the joint resolutions that propose constitutional amendments; other resolutions and special sessions are left out. "Vehicle bills" (empty placeholders filed so they can be rewritten later) are shown but left out of the statistics. Topics come from the Legislative Services Agency's subject index and the committees a bill was sent to, so a bill can have more than one.

“What it does” is the Legislative Services Agency's digest of the latest version of the bill. Votes are totals only: the data doesn't say how each member voted. Phone numbers are the caucus offices' shared lines.

Governor's deadlines are estimates: the governor has 7 days after a bill is presented, and the records show when the presiding officers signed it, not when it was presented.

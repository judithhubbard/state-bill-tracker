The steps

  1. Introduction. The General Assembly (150 representatives and 30 senators, all elected every two years) sits for a biennium: it convenes in January of the odd year (2025–26 is one biennium) and meets each year from January until May or June. Bills are numbered once for the biennium (H.1, S.1), stay alive through both years and die when the biennium ends. Most districts elect more than one member: a House district one or two representatives, a Senate district (a county or part of one) one to three senators.
  2. Committee. A bill goes to a committee of its own house, which takes testimony and votes to report it favorably, usually with amendments. Committees rarely vote a bill down: one they don't take up stays there until the biennium ends. A bill that spends money then goes to Appropriations, and one that raises revenue to Ways and Means (House) or Finance (Senate). Committees also write bills of their own, which start on the floor.
  3. Floor. A reported bill goes on the notice calendar, then the action calendar. The second reading is the main debate: the house votes on the committee's amendments and orders the bill to a third reading, which usually passes it the next day. Most votes are voice votes; any member can ask for a roll call (the "yeas and nays").
  4. The other house. The other house sends the bill to its own committee and passes it "in concurrence", or passes it with a "proposal of amendment". The first house then concurs, concurs with a further amendment (sending it back), or asks for a committee of conference, whose report both houses have to adopt.
  5. The governor. Bills that pass both houses are delivered to the governor, often in batches weeks after they pass. The governor has 5 days, Sundays excepted, to sign or veto a bill; one the governor doesn't act on becomes law without a signature (unless the General Assembly adjourns within three days of delivering it). The General Assembly usually comes back for a day in June to take up vetoes: overriding one takes two-thirds of the members present in each house. Laws are numbered as Acts through the biennium ("Act 44"); acts for one town or city, mostly charter changes, are municipal acts ("Act M-3").
  6. Constitutional amendments. The Senate proposes amendments to the Vermont Constitution ("Proposals"), which have to pass both houses in two successive bienniums and then the voters. They aren't in the data this site uses, so they aren't shown.
  7. The calendar. Most new laws take effect July 1; some take effect when signed or on a date the bill sets.

How to have an impact

  • Get the bill taken up. Most bills die in committee without a vote. Ask your legislators and the bill's sponsors to push the committee's chair to take it up, and write the committee's members.
  • Testify or write the committee. Committees meet in the State House in Montpelier, stream their meetings online and take written testimony; their agendas are posted the week before.
  • Move early. Bills generally have to be voted out of committee by "crossover" in mid-March to be taken up by the other house that year.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You may have more than one representative and up to three senators, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to sign on as a co-sponsor and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your town carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to take it up in the second year, or to introduce it again in the next biennium.

About this data

Where the data comes from: LegiScan, which collects the Vermont General Assembly's bills, histories, sponsors, roll-call votes and legislators and publishes them under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license; bill data on this site is from LegiScan, updated weekly, and more often while the General Assembly meets. (The Legislature's own website asks automated readers not to use it.) Who holds each seat now, with party and email, comes from Open States' public-domain list of legislators (it lists Progressives who also run as Democrats as "Democratic/Progressive"); State House phone numbers from LegiScan; towns and cities by district from the Census Bureau's district relationship files (by land area, so a town split between districts shows each one's share of its land, not of its people).

House and Senate bills are shown; resolutions are left out. Topics come from the committees a bill went to and the words of its title, so a bill can have more than one. There are no summaries: the title is the bill's official title ("An act relating to ..."), and the Legislative Counsel's statement of purpose is in the bill's text, linked from each bill.

Stages are worked out from each bill's history, and laws from their Act numbers. Votes are shown member by member where the house took a roll call (most votes in Vermont are voice votes): on ordering a bill to a third reading, passing it, concurring, adopting a conference report, and overriding a veto. The data has no committee members or chairs, so contact cards don't name a committee's chair. Governor's deadlines are estimates: 5 days, Sundays excepted, after the bill is delivered.

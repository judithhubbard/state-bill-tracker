The steps

  1. Introduction. A legislator introduces a bill, often at the request of a state agency, an interim committee or a group; the Legislative Services Division drafts it first (each draft has an "LC" number). Each Legislature lasts two years (the 69th is 2025–26), but it meets in regular session only in odd-numbered years: from early January, for at most 90 legislative days, to late April or early May. Bills that don't pass die when the session adjourns.
  2. Committee. The Speaker or the Senate President sends the bill to a standing committee on its subject. Every bill gets a public hearing, then the committee votes ("executive action"): "do pass" (in the second house, "be concurred in"), often with amendments, or to table it. Most bills that die are tabled. A majority of the full house can take a bill from a committee and put it on the floor, though that seldom succeeds.
  3. The floor. The full house debates the bill and votes on second reading, where it can be amended or killed ("indefinitely postponed"), then passes it on third reading, usually a day or more later. A bill that fails is dead unless the house reconsiders the vote.
  4. Deadlines. Bills have to pass their first house by the transmittal deadline, about halfway through the session (general bills in early March; bills about revenue or spending, and referenda, a few weeks later). Bills that miss it die.
  5. The other house. The bill gets a hearing, a committee vote and second and third reading again. If the second house amends it, the first has to agree ("concur"); if it won't, a conference committee (or a free conference committee, which can change more) works out a version both houses have to adopt.
  6. The governor. The bill is enrolled, signed by the Speaker and the Senate President and delivered to the governor, sometimes weeks after the session ends. The governor has 10 days to sign it, veto it, or send it back with proposed amendments ("amendatory veto"), which the Legislature can accept or reject. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. The governor can veto items in an appropriation bill. Laws are numbered as chapters of the session's laws and most take effect October 1.
  7. Veto overrides. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of the members present in each house. Most vetoes come after the session ends: then the Secretary of State polls the legislators by mail, and an override takes two-thirds of each house's members (34 senators and 67 representatives).
  8. Constitutional amendments. The Legislature proposes an amendment to the Constitution of Montana with a bill. It skips the governor, and it needs 100 votes: two-thirds of all 150 legislators, both houses' votes counted together. Then it goes to the voters at the next general election.

How to have an impact

  • Testify at the hearing. Every bill gets one. Committees take testimony in person in Helena, remotely and in writing; register ahead on the Legislature's site.
  • Reach the committee before it votes. Most bills that die are tabled in committee, often the same week as the hearing.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state senator and one state representative, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them how they'll vote on second reading, where most floor fights happen.
  • Move quickly. The session is short and comes only every other year: bills move through each house in a few weeks, and the transmittal deadline comes halfway through.
  • Start between sessions. Interim committees study issues in the off year and request bills for the next session; legislators request their drafts in the fall before it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please support it in the Senate"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Montana Legislature's bill data service (api.legmt.gov, the one its Bill Explorer uses): each bill's history, sponsors, subjects and chapter number; for 2025, how each member voted on the floor and in committee, and the co-sponsors; the members and committees. Members' own phone numbers come from Open States' public-domain file, and cities, towns, communities and counties from the Census Bureau's district relationship files.

House and Senate bills of each regular session are shown, with the bills that propose constitutional amendments; joint resolutions and special sessions are left out. Topics come from the Legislature's subject index and words in a bill's short title, so a bill can have more than one.

There's no summary of what each bill does: each bill's title is its short title, and the bill's text and fiscal note are on the Legislature's site; follow the link to the bill. For 2023 the records give vote tallies only, not how each member voted.

The records mark some failed floor votes in 2025 as passed (ties, and votes with more noes than ayes); here a roll call decides. Governor's deadlines are estimates from the Constitution's rule (10 days after delivery).

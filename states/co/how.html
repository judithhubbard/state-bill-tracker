The steps

  1. Introduction. A member introduces a bill; each member may introduce only a limited number. The General Assembly meets each year for a regular session of at most 120 days, from the second Wednesday of January to early May. Bills that don't pass by the end of the session die.
  2. Committee. The Speaker or the Senate President sends the bill to a committee of reference, which must hear it; anyone can testify. The committee refers it on (to another committee or the floor), lays it over, or kills it by "postponing it indefinitely". Bills that cost money go on to the Appropriations Committee.
  3. Floor. The full house debates and amends the bill on second reading, then votes on third reading. It needs a majority of all members: 33 in the House, 18 in the Senate.
  4. The other house. The bill goes to the other house and starts again in committee. If the second house amends it, the first has to agree ("concur"), or a conference committee works out the differences.
  5. The governor. During the session, the governor has 10 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto a bill; bills that arrive at the end get 30 days after adjournment. The governor can veto items in an appropriation bill. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house.
  6. Constitutional amendments. The General Assembly can refer an amendment to the voters with a concurrent resolution, which needs two-thirds of each house; citizens can also put amendments on the ballot by petition. Those resolutions aren't shown here.
  7. The calendar. Most new laws take effect 90 days after the session ends (early August), unless the bill has a safety clause, which makes it take effect when signed but rules out a citizens' referendum on it.

How to have an impact

  • Testify at the hearing. Every bill gets a committee hearing, where anyone can testify in person or remotely, or send written testimony; the General Assembly's website lists the schedule.
  • Watch Appropriations. Bills with a cost often wait in the Appropriations Committee until the budget is set, and many die there in the last weeks.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor a bill and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to bring it back next session.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the General Assembly's website (each bill's history, sponsors, subjects, summary and votes; the members and committees), updated daily, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, towns and counties.

House and Senate bills of the regular sessions are shown; resolutions (including those referring constitutional amendments to the voters) and special sessions are left out. Topics come from the General Assembly's subjects and the committees a bill was sent to, so a bill can have more than one.

“What it does” is the bill summary written by Legislative Council Staff. Votes list how each member voted, in committee and on the floor.

Governor's deadlines are estimates from the Constitution's rule (10 days during the session, Sundays excepted, and 30 days after it).

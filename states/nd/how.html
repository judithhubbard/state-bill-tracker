The steps

  1. Introduction. A legislator, a committee, or a committee at a state agency's request files a bill. Each Legislative Assembly lasts two years (the 69th is 2025–26), but it meets in regular session only in the first: from early January, for at most 80 legislative days, to late April or early May. Bills that don't pass die when the session adjourns. The Legislative Management or the governor can call a special session, which takes up only the bills it's called for (numbered after the regular session's).
  2. Committee. The bill goes to a standing committee on its subject. Every bill gets a public hearing, and the committee can't hold it: it reports the bill to the floor, recommending "do pass" or "do not pass", often with amendments. Bills that spend money usually go on to the Appropriations Committee.
  3. The floor. Every reported bill gets a vote of the full house, called second reading here, whatever the committee recommended. It needs a majority of all members: 48 in the House, 24 in the Senate. A bill that fails is dead, unless the house reconsiders the vote (usually the next day).
  4. The other house. The bill gets a hearing, a committee report and a floor vote again. If the second house amends it, the first has to agree ("concur") and pass it again; if it won't, a conference committee works out a version, which both houses have to adopt and pass.
  5. The governor. The Speaker and the Senate's presiding officer sign the bill and send it to the governor, who has 3 days (Sundays excepted) during the session, or 15 days after it adjourns, to sign or veto. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. The governor can veto items in an appropriation bill. Laws are numbered as chapters of the Session Laws and take effect August 1, unless the bill says otherwise.
  6. Veto overrides. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house's members (63 in the House, 32 in the Senate). Most vetoes come at the session's end, when there's still time to try.
  7. Constitutional amendments. The Legislative Assembly proposes an amendment to the Constitution of North Dakota with a concurrent resolution (HCR or SCR). It skips the governor: a majority of each house's members sends it to the voters at a statewide election.
  8. The Lieutenant Governor. The Lieutenant Governor presides over the Senate and isn't a senator; the Senate's majority leader runs its floor.

How to have an impact

  • Testify at the hearing. Every bill gets one. Committees take testimony in person in Bismarck, online, and in writing; written testimony goes into the bill's record on the Legislature's site.
  • Ask for the recommendation you want. The committee's "do pass" or "do not pass" carries weight on the floor: about nine in ten bills it recommends against fail, and about nine in ten it recommends pass.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state senator and two state representatives, and they listen most to their own constituents. Every bill comes to a floor vote, so ask them how they'll vote.
  • Move quickly. The session is short: bills move through each house in a few weeks, and there's no second year to catch up.
  • Start between sessions. Interim committees study issues in the off year and recommend bills for the next session.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please support it in the Senate"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the North Dakota Legislative Council's data files (each bill's history, sponsors, hearings and how each member voted on the floor; the members, committees and leaders), the Session Laws' bill index for chapter numbers, the members' biography pages for phones and emails, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, communities and counties.

House and Senate bills are shown, including those of special sessions, with the concurrent resolutions that propose constitutional amendments; other resolutions are left out. Bills have no short titles here, so each one's title is the first subject in its official title ("relating to ..."), or, for appropriations, what the money is for; its summary is the official title. Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and words in its title, so a bill can have more than one.

"What it does" is the Legislative Council's summary of the bills passed in a session (its Title Summaries, published some months after the session), so only bills that passed have one; budget bills have one only for the parts that change other laws. Committees' votes are tallies, shown in the bill's history; how each member voted in committee is in the committee's minutes.

Governor's deadlines are estimates from the Constitution's rule (3 days during the session, Sundays excepted, and 15 days after it).

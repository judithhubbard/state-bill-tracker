The steps

  1. Introduction. A member of the Assembly or the Senate introduces a bill, drafted by the Legislative Counsel, who also writes the digest at its top that says what it would do. It can't be voted on until it has been in print for 30 days.
  2. Policy committee. Each house's Rules Committee sends the bill to a committee on its subject, sometimes two. The committee hears it, takes public testimony, and votes. Most bills pass their policy committee, often with amendments.
  3. Appropriations. A bill that would cost the state money goes next to the Appropriations Committee. Most go on its “suspense file” and are decided together in one hearing, in May for bills in their own house and in August for bills from the other house, often with no discussion. Many bills die there, “held in committee” without a vote.
  4. Floor. A bill the committees approve goes to the floor of its house. Most need a majority: 41 votes in the Assembly, 21 in the Senate. Urgency bills, taxes and some others need two-thirds.
  5. The other house. The bill goes to the other house under the same number and goes through its committees and floor. If the second house amends it, it goes back to the first house, which votes on whether to agree to the changes (“concurrence”).
  6. The governor. A bill that passes both houses goes to the governor, who has 12 days to sign or veto it. For bills passed in the last days of each year, the governor has until mid-October in the first year and September 30 in the second. A bill the governor neither signs nor vetoes becomes law. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house, which almost never happens.
  7. Constitutional amendments. The Legislature proposes amendments to the state constitution as Assembly or Senate constitutional amendments (ACA, SCA). Each needs two-thirds of each house's members, then goes to the voters at a statewide election, not to the governor. The deadlines for passing a bill in its own house don't apply to them.
  8. The two-year session. A session runs two years, from December of an even year to the end of August two years later. A bill introduced in the first year that doesn't pass its own house that year can still pass it by January 31 of the second. Bills that don't pass both houses by August 31 of the second year die. New laws usually take effect on January 1.

How to have an impact

  • Act at the committee hearing. Committees post their agendas days ahead, and position letters are due about a week before the hearing (most committees take them through the Legislature's online portal). Members of the public can also speak briefly at the hearing.
  • Watch the suspense file. For a bill that costs money, the Appropriations hearings in May and August are where it lives or dies. Write the committee's chair and members, and the leaders, before then.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one Assembly member and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-author a bill and to vote for it.
  • Contact whoever decides the next step. In committee, that's the chair and members. Once a bill has passed both houses, it's the governor, who gets thousands of letters on the biggest bills in September.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the Senate; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the authors to introduce it again next session.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Legislature's database downloads (every bill, its versions, history, authors, votes, committee hearings and the governor's veto messages), and the Assembly and Senate websites for legislators and committees.

Assembly and Senate bills and constitutional amendments from the regular session are shown; other resolutions and special sessions' bills are left out. Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and the words in its title, so a bill can have more than one.

“What it does” is the Legislative Counsel's Digest of the latest version of the bill.

Governor's deadlines are estimates: 12 days after a bill reached the governor, or the end-of-year deadlines.

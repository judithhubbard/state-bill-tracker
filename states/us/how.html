The steps

  1. Introduction. A senator or representative introduces a bill ("H.R." in the House, "S." in the Senate) or a joint resolution ("H.J.Res.", "S.J.Res."), which works the same way. A Congress lasts two years (2025–26 is the 119th) and every bill dies when it ends. The House has 435 voting members, plus six delegates who can vote in committee but not on a bill's passage; the Senate has 100, two from each state.
  2. Committee. The Speaker or the Senate's presiding officer refers the bill to a committee, and in the House often to several. A subcommittee may hold hearings; the committee "marks up" the bill (amends it) and votes to report it. Most bills are never taken up: fewer than one in ten is reported. The House can take a bill away from its committee with a discharge petition signed by 218 members.
  3. The House floor. The Speaker and the majority leader decide what comes up. Most bills pass "under suspension of the rules": 40 minutes of debate, no amendments, and a two-thirds vote of those voting. Bigger bills come up under a rule written by the Rules Committee, which sets the debate and the amendments allowed, and pass by a majority.
  4. The Senate floor. The majority leader moves to take a bill up. Most pass by unanimous consent, often without a recorded vote; any senator can object. A contested bill needs 60 votes to end debate (cloture), first on taking it up and then on the bill itself, before it passes by a majority. Budget reconciliation bills can't be filibustered.
  5. Agreeing on one version. If the second house passes the bill with changes, the first can agree, change it again (the bill goes back and forth), or ask for a conference committee, whose report both houses then vote on. Often one house passes the other's bill instead of its own, and big bills carry the text of many smaller ones.
  6. The President. The bill is enrolled, signed by the Speaker and the President of the Senate, and presented to the President, who has 10 days, Sundays excepted, to sign it or veto it. A bill the President doesn't return becomes law without a signature, unless Congress has adjourned and can't receive the veto (a pocket veto). Laws are numbered "Public Law 119-21"; a private law helps a named person.
  7. Veto overrides. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house, voting in turn, starting with the house where the bill began. Most vetoes stand.
  8. The budget. The fiscal year starts October 1. The twelve appropriations bills are due by then; when they aren't passed, Congress passes a stopgap "continuing resolution" or the government shuts down. The President's budget request comes in February.
  9. Constitutional amendments. They're joint resolutions. One has to pass each house by two-thirds; it doesn't go to the President. Three-fourths of the states (38) then have to ratify it, usually within seven years.

How to have an impact

  • Start with your own members. You have one representative and two senators. Their offices keep track of what constituents say, and they listen most to people who live in their district or state.
  • Ask them to cosponsor. A bill with many cosponsors from both parties is likelier to be taken up. Ask your representative and senators to sign on.
  • Call, or use the contact form. Members of Congress don't publish email addresses; their websites have a contact form, which asks for your address so the office knows you're a constituent. A phone call to the Washington or a district office counts too.
  • Write to the committee. Committee members and staff decide what gets a hearing and a markup. A short letter from someone affected carries weight, especially when the member represents you.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please support it in the Senate"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Ask others to write too. Ten people writing once counts for more than one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Government Publishing Office's bulk bill status files on govinfo.gov (each bill's actions, sponsors, cosponsors, committees, laws, subjects and summaries, as on congress.gov), the House Clerk's and the Senate's roll-call records for how each member voted, the public-domain congress-legislators data (github.com/unitedstates/congress-legislators) for members, offices, phones, contact forms and committees, and the Census Bureau's relationship files for the congressional districts of each place.

House and Senate bills and joint resolutions are shown; simple and concurrent resolutions, which don't make law, are left out. Joint resolutions proposing an amendment to the Constitution are marked as such. Each bill's title is its title on congress.gov (its short title when it has one); its summary is its official title. Topics come from the policy area the Congressional Research Service gives each bill and words in its titles and subject terms, so a bill can have more than one. Congress has two extra topics: defense, and foreign affairs (including trade and immigration).

"What it does" is the Congressional Research Service's latest summary of the bill, labelled with the version it describes. Long summaries are cut short; the full text is on congress.gov.

Floor votes are the roll calls on the bill itself: passage, suspension of the rules, agreeing to the other house's changes, conference reports and veto overrides, and in the Senate the cloture votes and motions to take the bill up. Most bills pass by voice vote or unanimous consent, with no roll call. Some votes show only totals for now. Committee votes are the totals the House's committees report; no names.

Companion bills (the same bill introduced in each house) are separate records here, as on congress.gov; "Similar bills" shows them side by side. When one bill's text passes as part of another, its history says so.

The delegates of the District of Columbia, Guam, the Virgin Islands, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands and Puerto Rico's Resident Commissioner sponsor bills and vote in committee, but not on a bill's passage; they aren't counted in the House's party balance.

The steps

  1. Introduction. A senator, a delegate, a committee or the governor ("by request of the Administration") introduces a bill; many are prefiled in the fall. Many bills are introduced in both houses at once as "cross-files". The General Assembly meets for 90 days a year, from January to early April, and bills that don't pass by then die.
  2. Committee. The bill goes to a standing committee, which holds a hearing on it, where anyone can testify, then votes it favorable, favorable with amendments, or unfavorable. Many bills that won't pass are withdrawn by their sponsors.
  3. Floor. A bill the committee approves gets a second reading, where the house adopts amendments, and a third reading, where it votes to pass it.
  4. The other house. The bill starts again in a committee of the other house. If that house amends it, the first has to agree, or a conference committee works out the differences.
  5. The governor. Bills passed at the end of the session go to the governor within 20 days, and the governor has 30 days to sign or veto them (or veto items in the budget). A bill the governor doesn't veto becomes law. Overriding a veto takes three-fifths of each house.
  6. Constitutional amendments need three-fifths of each house and then go to the voters at the next general election, not to the governor.

How to have an impact

  • Testify at the hearing. Sign up on the General Assembly's website to testify in person or online, or send written testimony, usually a few days before the hearing.
  • Act early. The session is only 90 days, and most hearings are in its first two months.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state senator and up to three delegates, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor a bill and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House of Delegates; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to introduce it again next year.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the General Assembly's bill lists (each bill's sponsors, committees, readings, synopsis and status) and its member pages, updated daily, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, towns, communities and counties.

Senate bills and House bills are shown; joint resolutions and special sessions are left out. Topics come from the General Assembly's own subject headings and the words in a bill's title, so a bill can have more than one.

“What it does” is the bill's synopsis, written by the Department of Legislative Services. The bill lists don't give vote counts or the dates of the governor's actions; each bill's page has its votes.

The steps

  1. Introduction. A legislator files a bill, which passes "first reading" and is sent to a committee. A General Assembly lasts two years: a long session from January of the odd year and a short session in the even year. Bills stay alive through both, but most must pass one house by the crossover deadline.
  2. Committee. Every Senate bill is first sent to the Senate Rules Committee, which decides whether it goes on to a committee on its subject. A referral like "Judiciary, if favorable, Rules" sends a bill through several committees in turn; each reports it favorably, often as a committee substitute.
  3. Floor. A reported bill goes on the calendar, then passes second and third readings, usually the same day; a bill that raises revenue needs them on separate days.
  4. The other house. The bill starts again in a committee of the other house. If that house changes it, the first has to concur, or a conference committee works out the differences. Both presiding officers then ratify it.
  5. The governor. A public bill goes to the governor, who has 10 days to sign or veto it while the General Assembly is in session, and 30 days after it adjourns. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. Overriding a veto takes three-fifths of each house's members present and voting.
  6. Local bills and constitutional amendments don't go to the governor. A local bill (for one city or county) becomes law when ratified. A constitutional amendment passes both houses by three-fifths of their members, then goes to the voters at the election the act names.

How to have an impact

  • Ask the committee chairs. North Carolina's committees often have several co-chairs; they decide which bills get a hearing. Write them and the members, and ask your own legislators to talk to them.
  • Mind crossover. Most bills that haven't passed one house by the crossover deadline are dead for the session.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor a bill and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the General Assembly's website (each bill's history, sponsors and roll calls; the members and committees), updated daily; legislators' emails from Open States, since the General Assembly's pages hide them; and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, towns and counties.

House and Senate bills are shown, including constitutional amendments, which North Carolina passes as bills; resolutions and extra sessions are left out. Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to, its official keywords and the words in its title, so a bill can have more than one.

The General Assembly's bill summaries are PDF files, so “What it does” isn't shown; each bill's page links to them. Floor votes list how each member voted.

Governor's deadlines are estimates from the Constitution's rule (10 days during the session, 30 after it adjourns).

The steps

  1. Introduction. A member, or a committee, introduces a bill. The General Assembly meets each year: a long session in odd years (January to early June) and a short one in even years (February to early May). Bills that don't pass by the end of the session die.
  2. Committee. Connecticut's committees are joint: each has members from both the House and the Senate. The committee holds a public hearing, where anyone can testify, then votes on a "Joint Favorable" report. Each committee has a deadline to report its bills. Bills that cost money go on to Appropriations or Finance, Revenue and Bonding.
  3. Floor. A reported bill goes on the House or Senate calendar. The full house debates and votes; the leaders decide which bills come up, and many pass on a consent calendar.
  4. The other house. The bill goes to the other house, which can pass it as is or amend it; an amended bill goes back to the first house to agree.
  5. The governor. A bill that passes both houses gets a Public Act number and goes to the governor through the Secretary of the State. The governor has 5 days (Sundays and holidays excepted) during the session, or 15 days after it adjourns, to sign or veto it, and can veto items in a budget bill. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house.
  6. The calendar. Most new laws take effect October 1 or July 1.
  7. Constitutional amendments are joint resolutions (HJ, SJ), and they don't go to the governor. One adopted by three-fourths of the members of each house goes to the voters at the next general election, in November of an even year. One adopted by a majority but less than three-fourths has to be adopted again, by a majority, by the Legislature elected next; then it goes to the voters.

How to have an impact

  • Testify at the public hearing. Every bill a committee takes up gets a public hearing, where anyone can testify in person or remotely, or send written testimony. It's the main chance to be heard before the committee votes.
  • Mind the deadlines. Each committee must report its bills by a deadline, and the session ends on a fixed day. A bill that misses either dies for the year.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor a bill and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors or the committee to introduce it again next year.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the General Assembly's public data files (each bill's title, statement of purpose, history, sponsors and committee referrals; the legislators and committees), updated several times a day, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for towns.

House and Senate bills are shown, and joint resolutions proposing amendments to the State Constitution, tagged “Constitutional amendments”; other resolutions are left out. Amendments have no statement of purpose, so “What it does” isn't shown for them, and the data files don't say how many members voted for one, only when a house adopted it by less than three-fourths. Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and the words in its title and statement of purpose, so a bill can have more than one.

“What it does” is the bill's statement of purpose. The data files don't include votes; each bill's page on the General Assembly's site has them.

Governor's deadlines are estimates from the Constitution's rule (5 days during the session, 15 after it).

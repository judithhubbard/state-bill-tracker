The steps

  1. Introduction. A senator, a representative or, most often, a committee introduces a bill; many are prefiled before January. The Legislature meets in two-year bienniums (2025–26), with a session each year from the second Monday of January to "first adjournment" in late March or early April, then a few days in late April for the veto session. Bills stay alive through both years and die when the biennium ends. The Senate has 40 members and the House 125.
  2. Committee. The bill goes to a standing committee, which holds a hearing and recommends it be passed, often with amendments, or as a substitute bill that keeps the number. An uncontroversial bill can go on the consent calendar. A bill the chair doesn't bring up stays in committee. Bills are often moved from one committee to another, or sent back from the calendar to a committee. Most bills have to pass their own house by "turnaround" in late February, except those in exempt committees such as Appropriations, Ways and Means, Taxation and Federal and State Affairs.
  3. General Orders. A reported bill goes on the calendar under General Orders, where the whole house sits as the Committee of the Whole, debates it, amends it, and recommends it be passed by a voice vote.
  4. Final action. The roll call that passes the bill comes on a later day ("final action"), or the same day, if the house first votes to take it up at once ("emergency final action"). A bill on the consent calendar passes after three days without debate, unless a member objects and it goes to General Orders.
  5. The other house. The bill goes through a committee, General Orders and final action again. If the second house changes it, the first has to agree ("concur"); if it won't, a conference committee of three members from each house works out one version, and both houses have to adopt its report. Conference reports often carry the contents of other bills too.
  6. The governor. A bill that passed both houses is enrolled and presented to the governor, who has 10 days to sign or veto it; a bill not returned becomes law without the signature. The governor can veto items in an appropriation bill.
  7. Veto overrides. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of the members of each house: 27 senators and 84 representatives, starting with the house where the bill began. Line-item vetoes are overridden item by item. Most overrides come in the veto session in late April.
  8. The budget. The fiscal year starts July 1. The governor proposes a budget in January; the House Appropriations and Senate Ways and Means committees and their budget committees write the appropriations bills, which are usually settled in conference before first adjournment, with the last changes in the veto session.
  9. Constitutional amendments. They're concurrent resolutions. One has to pass each house by two-thirds of its members; it doesn't go to the governor. It goes to the voters at the next general election, or at the election the resolution names, and becomes part of the constitution if a majority approves.

How to have an impact

  • Testify at the committee hearing. Committees take testimony from proponents, opponents and neutral parties, in person or in writing, at the Statehouse in Topeka. A bill the committee doesn't report goes no further.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state senator and one state representative, and they listen most to their own constituents.
  • Mind the calendar. Most bills have to pass their own house by turnaround in late February. Bills don't die at the end of the first year, so a bill that stalled can move in the second.
  • Watch the conference committees. Late in the session, many bills pass as part of a conference committee's report on another bill. Ask your legislators what's in the report before they vote.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please support it in the Senate"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Kansas Legislature's public data service, kslegislature.gov/api/v1 (each bill's status and history, its sponsors and versions, the governor's actions, every roll call and how each member voted, the Legislative Research Department's supplemental notes, the members and the committees), and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, communities and counties.

House and Senate bills are shown, with the concurrent resolutions proposing constitutional amendments; other resolutions are left out. Each bill's title is its short title; its summary is its official title, without the list of sections it changes. Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and words in its titles and the Research Department's keywords, so a bill can have more than one.

"What it does" is the Kansas Legislative Research Department's supplemental note on the bill's latest version: its brief, then its background (the hearings and the committees' changes). Supplemental notes don't express legislative intent. Notes for 2023–24 aren't included yet.

Floor votes are roll calls on the bill itself: final action, the consent calendar, concurrence, conference reports and veto overrides. Member-by-member votes are filled in as they're downloaded; until then a vote shows its totals. Committees in Kansas don't take roll calls.

Most Kansas bills are introduced by committees, often at someone's request; the committee is shown as the sponsor. The Legislature lists sponsors by last name, so two members who share one (Carpenter, Smith, Ruiz) are told apart only when the member's own page lists the bill.

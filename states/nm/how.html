The steps

  1. Introduction. The Legislature meets once a year from the third Tuesday of January: 60 days in odd years, and 30 in even years, when it may take up only the budget, bills the governor puts on the call and bills vetoed the year before. Members can prefile bills in December. Bills that don't pass by the end of the session die.
  2. Committees. The presiding officer sends each bill to the committees on its subject, usually two, and to a finance committee last if it spends money; it has to get out of each in turn. A committee recommends "do pass" (often with amendments, or with its own substitute bill), "do not pass" or no recommendation, or tables the bill. In 30-day sessions the Senate Committees' Committee first decides whether each Senate bill is germane.
  3. Floor. A bill its last committee reports goes on the calendar, and the full house debates, amends and votes on it. A majority of those voting passes it.
  4. The other house. The bill goes through the other house's committees and floor. If that house changes it, the first house has to agree ("concur"); if it refuses, the other house can drop its changes ("recede"), or a conference committee settles the differences and both houses adopt its report.
  5. The governor. During the session the governor has 3 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto a bill, or it becomes law without a signature. Most bills pass in the last days, and for those the governor has 20 days after the session ends: a bill not signed by then is vetoed (a "pocket veto"). The governor can veto items in an appropriation bill (a "line-item" or "partial" veto). Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house, which rarely happens. Laws are numbered chapters of each year's session laws ("Laws 2025, chapter 13"); most take effect 90 days after the session.
  6. Constitutional amendments. Amendments to the state constitution are joint resolutions (HJR, SJR). A majority of each house's members sends one to the voters at the next general election (or a special election the Legislature calls), never to the governor.

How to have an impact

  • Get the bill heard. Most bills that fail are never taken up by one of their committees before the session runs out. Ask your legislators and the bill's sponsor to press the committee chair, and write the committee's members yourself.
  • Testify. Committees meet in the Roundhouse in Santa Fe, and most allow public comment in person or online over Zoom; the Legislature's website lists each day's hearings.
  • Move quickly. The sessions are short (30 days in even years), and a bill can go from introduction to the governor in a few weeks.
  • Write the governor early. Most bills reach the governor in the session's last days, and the governor then has 20 days; a bill not signed by the deadline dies.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state senator and one state representative, and they listen most to their own constituents.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask a legislator to prefile it for next session (in an even year, only the governor can put a new bill on the call).

About this data

Where the data comes from: the New Mexico Legislature's website for each regular session (each bill's page with its sponsors and actions, the floor roll calls, the committee reports with their roll calls, the Legislative Finance Committee's fiscal impact reports and the governor's veto messages), the Legislature's member and committee pages, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for places and counties.

House and Senate bills of each regular session are shown, with the joint resolutions that propose constitutional amendments; memorials, other resolutions and special sessions are left out. Appropriation bills are marked "Budget bill". Topics come from the committees a bill went to, or else from its title and synopsis, so a bill can have more than one.

"What it does" is the synopsis in the Legislative Finance Committee's fiscal impact report: the bill's (or its newest committee substitute's), then the later amendments'. Bills without a report have none.

Floor votes are member by member from the Legislature's roll calls (the final vote in each house), and committee votes from the committee reports, which name the members voting no, excused or absent. Members who have left the Legislature are shown by name.

Laws are bills with a chapter in that year's session laws: signed by the governor, or law without a signature. They, the vetoes and the pocket vetoes match the Legislature's own counts.

The steps

  1. Co-sponsorship memo and introduction. Before introducing a bill, a member usually circulates a memo asking colleagues to co-sponsor it. The bill is then introduced in the House or Senate. A session lasts two years, and bills stay alive until it ends November 30 of the second year.
  2. Committee. The Speaker or the Senate's President Pro Tempore sends the bill to a standing committee. The committee's majority chair decides whether it gets a hearing or a vote; the committee reports it "as committed" (unchanged) or "as amended".
  3. Floor. Each bill is considered three times on separate days. The House often tables a reported bill and takes it off the table when it's ready to move. Most bills go to the Appropriations Committee before third consideration, when the house votes on final passage.
  4. The other house. The bill starts again in a committee of the other house. If that house amends it, the first has to concur, or a conference committee works out a compromise.
  5. The governor. The Speaker and the Senate's presiding officer sign the bill, and it's presented to the governor, who has 10 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto it, or 30 days after the session ends. The governor can veto items in a spending bill. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house.
  6. Constitutional amendments are joint resolutions. They pass both houses in two successive sessions, then go to the voters, never to the governor.

How to have an impact

  • Ask the majority chair. A bill moves only if the committee's majority chair schedules it. Write the chair and the committee members, and ask your own legislators to talk to them.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor a bill and to vote for it.
  • Watch Appropriations. Most bills pass through it just before the final vote; a bill can wait there a long time.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to introduce it again next session.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the General Assembly's bill history files (each bill's sponsors and actions) and its roll-call pages (how each member voted, in committee and on the floor), updated daily; its member and committee feeds; emails from Open States; and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, boroughs and townships.

House and Senate bills of the regular session are shown, including joint resolutions proposing constitutional amendments; simple resolutions and special sessions are left out. A bill's title is its co-sponsorship memo's title where it has one. Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and the words in its title, so a bill can have more than one.

“What it does” is the brief description the committee's roll call gives, for bills a committee voted on.

Governor's deadlines are estimates from the Constitution's rule (10 days, Sundays excepted).

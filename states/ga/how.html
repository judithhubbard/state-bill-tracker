The steps

  1. Introduction. A member drops a bill in the "hopper", and it's read for the first time. A General Assembly lasts two years; each year's session is 40 legislative days, from January to late March or early April. Bills that don't pass in the first year carry over to the second.
  2. Committee. The Speaker or the Lieutenant Governor sends the bill to a standing committee. Its chair decides whether it gets a hearing and a vote; the committee reports it "do pass" (often as a substitute) or holds it.
  3. Rules and the floor. A bill the committee approves goes to the Rules Committee, which decides which bills reach the floor. The full house then votes on third reading.
  4. The other house. The bill starts again in a committee of the other house. If that house changes it, the first has to agree, or a conference committee works out the differences. "Crossover Day", about day 28, is the last day for a bill to pass its first house and still be sure of a vote in the other that year.
  5. The governor. During the session the governor has 6 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto a bill; bills that arrive in the last days get 40 days after the session ends. The governor can veto items in the budget. A bill the governor doesn't veto becomes law. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house.
  6. Local bills. Bills for one city or county pass on a local calendar, usually without debate, when that area's delegation agrees.

How to have an impact

  • Ask the committee chair. A bill moves only if the chair gives it a hearing and a vote. Write the chair and members, and ask your own legislators to talk to them.
  • Mind Crossover Day. A bill that hasn't passed its first house by then usually waits until next year, or dies.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor a bill and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to introduce it again in the next General Assembly.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the General Assembly's web services (each bill's sponsors, committees, status history and floor votes; the members and committees), updated daily, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities and counties.

House and Senate bills of the regular sessions are shown; resolutions and special sessions are left out. Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and the words in its title, so a bill can have more than one.

“What it does” is drawn from the bill's official title (what it does "so as to"); Georgia doesn't publish plain-language summaries. Floor votes list how each member voted; committee votes aren't published.

Governor's deadlines are estimates from the Constitution's rule (6 days during the session, 40 days after it).

The steps

  1. Introduction. A member files a bill. A General Assembly lasts two years; each year's session is 40 legislative days, from January to late March or early April. Bills that don't pass in the first year carry over to the second.
  2. Committee. The Speaker or the Lieutenant Governor sends the bill to a standing committee. Its chair decides whether it gets a hearing and a vote; the committee approves it (often rewritten) or holds it.
  3. Rules and the floor. A bill the committee approves goes to the Rules Committee, which decides which bills reach the floor. The full house then votes.
  4. The other house. The bill starts again in a committee of the other house. If that house changes it, the first has to agree, or a conference committee works out the differences. "Crossover Day", about day 28, is the last day for a bill to pass its first house and still be sure of a vote in the other that year.
  5. The governor. During the session the governor has 6 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto a bill; bills that arrive in the last days get 40 days after the session ends. The governor can veto items in the budget. A bill the governor doesn't veto becomes law. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house.
  6. Constitutional amendments › An amendment to the state Constitution is a House or Senate resolution ("HR 1022"). It needs two-thirds of the members of each house (120 in the House, 38 in the Senate), doesn't go to the governor for approval (it can't be vetoed), and goes to the voters at the next general election, in November of an even year.
  7. Local bills. Bills for one city or county pass on a local calendar, usually without debate, when that area's delegation agrees.

How to have an impact

  • Ask the committee chair. A bill moves only if the chair gives it a hearing and a vote. Write the chair and members, and ask your own legislators to talk to them.
  • Crossover Day. A bill that hasn't passed its first house by then usually waits until next year, or dies.
  • Bring in your own legislators: they listen most to their own voters. Ask them to co-sponsor, to press the chair or leaders, and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with news: a new fact, a local impact, a new stage.
  • Ask others to write too. Ten people writing once counts for more than one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to introduce it again in the next General Assembly.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the General Assembly's web services (each bill's sponsors, committees, status history and floor votes; the members and committees), updated daily, and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities and counties.

House and Senate bills of the regular sessions are shown, with the resolutions proposing amendments to the state Constitution; other resolutions and special sessions are left out. Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and the words in its title, so a bill can have more than one.

“What it does” is drawn from the bill's official title (what it does "so as to"); Georgia doesn't publish plain-language summaries. Floor votes list how each member voted; committee votes aren't published.

Governor's deadlines are estimates from the Constitution's rule (6 days during the session, 40 days after it).

The steps

  1. Introduction. A member asks the Office of Legislative Services to draft a bill (a "legislative service request"), and it's filed with a number. The General Court is elected every two years and meets each year from early January to the end of June. A bill that doesn't pass dies with its year, unless a committee keeps it over to the second year of the General Court.
  2. Committee. Every bill goes to a committee, which holds a public hearing and must report it to the full House or Senate: "ought to pass" (often with an amendment), "inexpedient to legislate" (kill it), "interim study" (also the end of it for the year), or, in the first year, "retained" (House) or "re-referred" (Senate) to work on it into the second. House bills that spend money go on to Finance, and those that change taxes or fees to Ways and Means.
  3. Floor. The full House or Senate votes on the committee's report, usually by voice; a report the committee agreed on goes on a consent calendar. Adopting "inexpedient to legislate" kills the bill. The House has 400 members and the Senate 24, and each house sets a deadline, in the spring, for acting on its own bills.
  4. The other house. The bill goes through committee and the floor again. If the second house amends it, the first house concurs, or asks for a committee of conference, whose report both houses have to adopt.
  5. The governor. Both houses adopt the enrolled bill, the Speaker and the Senate President sign it, and it's presented to the governor, who has 5 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto it. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law without a signature. There's no line-item veto. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of the members present in each house, usually on a "veto day" in the fall. Laws are numbered by chapter (Chapter 74, Laws of 2025).
  6. Constitutional amendments. A CACR (constitutional amendment concurrent resolution) proposes an amendment to the New Hampshire Constitution. It skips the governor: three-fifths of each house's members have to pass it, and then it goes to the voters at the next general election, where it needs two-thirds of the votes cast.
  7. The calendar. Most new laws take effect 60 days after they're signed, or on a date the bill sets (often July 1 or January 1).

How to have an impact

  • Testify at the hearing, or sign in. Every bill gets a public hearing. You can speak, or register your position online with the House or Senate before the hearing; committees count both.
  • Write to the committee before its vote. The committee's recommendation usually decides the floor vote, and most bills are settled on the consent calendar.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state senator and several state representatives (most towns elect more than one, and many also vote in a "floterial" district shared with neighboring towns). They listen most to their own constituents.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please support it in the Senate"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your town carries more weight than form text.
  • If it's retained or sent to interim study, the committee keeps working on it over the summer and fall: its work sessions are public, and that's when to weigh in.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the General Court's data files (each bill's docket, sponsors and status, and the House and Senate roll calls with each member's vote; earlier years from the General Court's public database in the same form), updated daily; its list of members (phones, offices and committees); and the districts as RSA 662:3 and 662:5 set them, for towns and city wards.

House and Senate bills of each year's session are shown, with CACRs (proposed constitutional amendments, shown as HCACR or SCACR by the house they start in); other resolutions are left out. A bill a committee kept over to the second year of the General Court appears on both years' pages. Topics come from each bill's subject code and the committees it was sent to, so a bill can have more than one.

“What it does” is the analysis at the top of the bill's latest text, written by the Office of Legislative Services; its label says which version it describes (an analysis written for the bill as introduced may not cover later amendments). Budget bills have none. Most votes are voice votes, with no names or totals; roll calls show how each member voted.

About a third of representatives list no phone of their own; email works for all of them.

Governor's deadlines are estimates: the governor has 5 days after a bill is presented, and the records show when both houses adopted the enrolled bill, not when it was presented.

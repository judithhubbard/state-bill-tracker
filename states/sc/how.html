The steps

  1. Introduction. A senator or representative files a bill, often prefiled in December. Each General Assembly lasts two years (the 126th is 2025–26), with a session each year from the second Tuesday of January to the second Thursday of May. Bills stay alive through both years and die when the General Assembly ends. The Senate has 46 members and the House 124.
  2. Committee. The bill goes to a standing committee, which often sends it to a subcommittee for a hearing first. The committee reports the bill favorably, with or without amendments; a bill it doesn't report stays there. Either house can recall a bill from committee, and a Senate committee's members can be polled in writing. Local bills, for one county or school district, go to that county's legislative delegation instead.
  3. The floor. A reported bill goes on the calendar. Its second reading is the main debate, with amendments and usually a roll call; its third reading, usually the next day, passes it. In the House, members who object to a bill can ask for debate on it, which takes it off the uncontested calendar.
  4. The other house. The bill goes through a committee and both readings again. If the second house amends it, the first has to agree; if it won't, a conference committee of three members from each house works out one version, and both houses have to adopt its report.
  5. Ratification. A bill that passed both houses in the same form is enrolled and then ratified: the President of the Senate and the Speaker sign it in a joint session, usually within days ("Ratified R 167" is its ratification number). Then it goes to the governor.
  6. The governor. The governor has 5 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto a bill; a bill not returned becomes law, and after the General Assembly adjourns the governor has until the second day it meets again. The governor can veto items in an appropriation bill. Laws are numbered as Acts through each General Assembly (Act No. 212 of 2026).
  7. Veto overrides. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of the members present and voting in each house, starting with the house where the bill began.
  8. The budget. The fiscal year starts July 1. The House Ways and Means Committee writes the general appropriations bill, which the House debates in March and the Senate in April; a conference committee settles it in May or June, after the regular session, under the sine die resolution that lets the houses come back.
  9. Constitutional amendments. They're joint resolutions. One has to pass each house by two-thirds of its members; it doesn't go to the governor. It goes to the voters at the next general election, and if they approve it, the next General Assembly ratifies it by an act, which makes it part of the constitution.
  10. Joint resolutions. Other joint resolutions have the force of law, like bills: most approve state agencies' regulations or make one-time rules. They're shown here with the bills.

How to have an impact

  • Speak at the subcommittee hearing. Most bills get their hearing in a subcommittee, at the State House complex in Columbia; it decides what the full committee sees. A bill the committee doesn't report goes no further.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state senator and one state representative, and they listen most to their own constituents.
  • Mind the calendar. A bill that hasn't passed its own house by April 10 needs two-thirds of the other house to be taken up that year. Bills don't die at the end of the first year, so a bill that stalled can move in the second.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please support it in the Senate"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.

About this data

Where the data comes from: South Carolina Legislature Online, scstatehouse.gov (each bill's record with its sponsors, official title and history, each roll call with how each member voted, the lists of Acts, the members' pages and the committees), and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, towns, communities and counties.

General bills and joint resolutions are shown, including the joint resolutions proposing constitutional amendments; concurrent resolutions and resolutions are left out. Each bill's title is its subject line; its summary is its official title. Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and words in its title, so a bill can have more than one. Bills sent to a county's legislative delegation, or amending a local act for one place, are marked as local bills.

"What it does" is taken from the bill's official title: the clauses ("so as to ...") that say what each change does, not a plain summary. The budget bills have none. South Carolina publishes plain summaries only for Acts, and they aren't included here. The title describes the bill as last amended.

Floor votes are roll calls, member by member: the second reading (the main vote), third readings, concurrences, conference reports and veto overrides that had one. Many third readings are voice votes. Committees' votes aren't published.

The Legislature doesn't publish legislators' email addresses; each one has a contact form on scstatehouse.gov, linked here. A member's phones are the Columbia office's and those on their page.

The member lists give each person's district in the office they hold now, so a few members who later moved to the other house (or to Congress) are placed in their district of the time by hand.

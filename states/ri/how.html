The steps

  1. Introduction. The General Assembly meets each year from the first Tuesday of January, usually until June. Senators and representatives serve two-year terms, but bills don't carry over: a bill that hasn't become law when the session ends has to be introduced again the next year. Most bills are introduced in both houses with the same text, a House bill and a Senate bill (companions); this site shows the two as one bill.
  2. Committee. Each bill goes to a committee in its own house, which holds a hearing. At the hearing the committee usually recommends the bill be “held for further study”, which is how most bills end. The bills the chair and the leaders want to move come back, often months later, for a vote to recommend passage, often as a substitute (“Sub A”) that rewrites it. Bills that cost money usually go to Finance.
  3. Floor. There's no Rules or Calendar Committee: the Speaker and the Senate President decide which bills go on the floor calendar (the consent calendar for bills nobody objects to). A bill passes with a majority of those voting.
  4. The other house. A bill that passes goes to the other house's committee and floor. When it passes there unchanged (“passed in concurrence”), it has passed both houses. If the second house changes it, it goes back to the first house, which has to pass the new version “in concurrence”. Companion bills usually pass both houses in the same version, and both go to the governor and become separate laws.
  5. The governor. The bill is transmitted to the governor, who has 6 days, Sundays excepted, to sign or veto it. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law without a signature (“effective without the governor's signature”). Overriding a veto takes three-fifths of the members present in each house. Laws are numbered as public laws (“Public Law 2025-123”).
  6. Constitutional amendments. The General Assembly proposes an amendment to the Rhode Island Constitution with a joint resolution, passed by a majority of each house's members. It skips the governor and goes to the voters at the next general election.
  7. The calendar. Most bills are introduced by early March. The budget usually passes in June, and the session ends soon after with many bills passed in its last days. Many new laws take effect July 1, the start of the state's budget year.

How to have an impact

  • Get the bill out of “further study”. Most bills are heard and held. Ask your legislators and the bill's sponsors to press the committee chair to bring it back for a vote.
  • Mind both versions. Most bills have a House and a Senate companion. Follow both, and write the committees in both houses.
  • Testify or write the committee. Committees meet at the State House in Providence; hearings are listed the week before and streamed on Capitol TV. Written testimony is accepted.
  • Watch the end of the session. Many bills move only in the last weeks, after the budget. That's when a call to your legislators counts most.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to sign on as a co-sponsor and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage (“it just passed the House; please sign it”). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to introduce it again next January.

About this data

Where the data comes from: LegiScan, which collects the Rhode Island General Assembly's bills, histories, sponsors, roll-call votes and legislators and publishes them under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license; bill data on this site is from LegiScan, updated weekly, and more often while the General Assembly meets. (The General Assembly's own website asks automated readers not to use it.) Who holds each seat now, with party and email, comes from Open States' public-domain list of legislators; State House phone numbers from LegiScan; cities and towns by district from the Census Bureau's district relationship files.

House and Senate bills of the regular sessions are shown, with the joint resolutions that propose constitutional amendments; other resolutions are left out, including joint resolutions that become law (most make grants to named organizations or set up study commissions). A House bill and a Senate bill with exactly the same description are shown together as one bill, its stage the furthest either reached; each number keeps its own history and votes. Both companions usually become law, each with its own public law number, so the official count of new laws is higher than the number of laws shown here. Topics come from the title of the General Laws a bill amends and the committees it went to, so a bill can have more than one.

“What it does” is the General Assembly's one-line description of the bill, as LegiScan publishes it. Floor votes and committee votes to recommend passage are shown member by member; Senate committees' votes to hold a bill for further study aren't. The data has no committee members or chairs, and no public law numbers.

Stages are worked out from each bill's history. Governor's deadlines are estimates: 6 days, Sundays excepted, after the bill is sent.

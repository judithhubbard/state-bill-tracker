The steps

  1. Introduction. Each Legislature lasts two years (the 132nd is 2025–26): it convenes in December after the election, holds a long first session through June, and a short second session from January to April, limited to budget bills, emergencies, carried-over bills and bills the Legislative Council allows. A bill is printed as a Legislative Document with an LD number ("LD 1328"), which it keeps in both houses. On this site the number carries the house where the bill started in front: HLD 1328 is LD 1328, a House paper; SLD 2 is LD 2, a Senate paper.
  2. Committee. Committees are joint: each bill goes to one Joint Standing Committee of senators and representatives, which holds a public hearing and a work session, then reports Ought to Pass (often "as amended"), Ought Not to Pass, or Leave to Withdraw. A committee can split, with a majority and a minority report. A unanimous Ought Not to Pass report ends the bill unless a member asks to take it up.
  3. Floor. The house where the bill started takes it up first; with a divided report, each house votes on which report to accept. A bill passes each house twice: "passed to be engrossed" approves its text after the second reading; once both houses agree on the text (the second house amending it, the first "receding and concurring", or a committee of conference), it's "passed to be enacted" by the House and then the Senate. Resolves and constitutional resolutions are "finally passed". On this site a house passes a bill when it passes it to be engrossed, and a bill has passed both houses when both have enacted it. Emergency bills need two-thirds of each house's members.
  4. The Appropriations Table. Bills that cost money are usually placed on the Senate's Special Appropriations Table after the House enacts them. At the end of the session the Appropriations Committee decides which to fund; the Senate enacts those, and the rest die on the table.
  5. The governor. The governor has 10 days, Sundays excepted, to sign or veto a bill; one she doesn't act on becomes law without a signature. When the Legislature adjourns first, she can hold a bill until three days after it meets again; if it never meets again, the bill doesn't become law (a pocket veto). Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of the members present in each house. Laws are numbered each year by kind: "Public Law 2025, chapter 12", private and special laws, and resolves ("Resolves 2025, chapter 3"). Laws without an emergency clause take effect 90 days after the session adjourns.
  6. Constitutional amendments. The Legislature proposes an amendment to the Constitution of Maine with a resolution (also an LD). It skips the governor: it needs two-thirds of each house, then the voters decide at the next statewide election in November.
  7. Tribal representatives. The Passamaquoddy Tribe and the Houlton Band of Maliseet Indians each send a representative to the House, who can sponsor bills and speak but doesn't vote on the floor (a third seat, the Penobscot Nation's, has been empty since the Nation withdrew its representative in 2015). They aren't tied to a district, so "Where you live" doesn't list them.

How to have an impact

  • Testify at the public hearing. Every bill gets a public hearing before its joint committee, in person, online or in writing. It's the best moment to be heard.
  • Watch the work session. The committee decides there, often with amendments. Write the committee's members before it meets.
  • Mind the money. A bill that costs money usually waits on the Special Appropriations Table until the session's end. Ask your legislators and the Appropriations Committee's members to fund it.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor it and to vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it was just enacted; please sign it"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • If it runs out of time, ask the sponsors to have it carried over to the second session, or to file it again in the next Legislature.

About this data

Where the data comes from: LegiScan, which collects the Maine Legislature's bills, histories, sponsors, roll-call votes and legislators and publishes them under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license; bill data on this site is from LegiScan, updated weekly, and more often while the Legislature meets. (The Legislature's own website asks automated readers not to use it.) Who holds each seat now, with party and email, comes from Open States' public-domain list of legislators; phone numbers from LegiScan; towns, plantations, unorganized territories and counties by district from the Census Bureau's district relationship files.

Bills and resolves (every Legislative Document) of the regular sessions are shown, with the resolutions that propose constitutional amendments; joint orders and other resolutions are left out. Topics come from the joint committee a bill went to and the words of its title, so a bill can have more than one.

Stages are worked out from each bill's history. The records don't show when a bill reached the governor or was signed, nor its chapter number: a bill enacted by both houses that LegiScan shows as chaptered (or passed) is shown as a law from the day of its final enactment. Vetoes come from the Legislature's votes on them. There are no summaries of what bills do, and no committee votes, committee members or chairs, so contact cards don't name a committee's chairs. Members' votes are those on the bill's own questions: accepting an "Ought to Pass" report, engrossment, enactment and veto overrides (a "yes" to accept an "Ought Not to Pass" report is a vote against the bill, and those aren't shown).

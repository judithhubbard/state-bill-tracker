The steps

  1. Introduction. A senator or representative files a bill. Each General Assembly lasts two years (the 153rd is 2025–26), with a session each year from the second Tuesday of January to June 30. Bills stay alive through both years and die when the General Assembly ends. The Senate has 21 members and the House 41.
  2. Committee. The Speaker or the Senate's President Pro Tempore sends the bill to a standing committee, which holds a public hearing. The committee releases the bill when most of its members sign the report, each one "favorable", "unfavorable" or "on its merits" (neither for nor against). A bill that doesn't get enough signatures stays in committee, and a committee can also table a bill. Bills that cost money go on to the House Appropriations or Senate Finance Committee.
  3. Substitutes. When a bill needs bigger changes than amendments, its sponsor can replace it with a substitute ("HS 1 for HB 457"), a new text that takes the bill's place, usually in its committee. This site shows a substituted bill under its own number, with the substitute's title, synopsis and history.
  4. The floor. A released bill goes on the ready list, and the house's majority leader decides when it comes up. Passing takes a majority of all members (21 in the House, 11 in the Senate), or more for some bills: three-fifths for a new or higher tax, three-fourths for money given to a town, a county or a private organization, as in the grants-in-aid bill. Every passage is a roll call.
  5. The other house. The bill goes through a committee and a floor vote again. If the second house amends it, the bill goes back to the first, which has to pass it again with the amendment.
  6. The governor. The General Assembly sends passed bills to the governor when it chooses, often weeks or months later. The governor has 10 days (Sundays excepted) to sign or veto a bill, and can veto items in an appropriation bill. A bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law, except after the General Assembly's term ends: then a bill not signed within 30 days doesn't become law (a pocket veto). Laws are numbered as chapters of the Laws of Delaware, one volume per General Assembly (Volume 85 is the 153rd's).
  7. Veto overrides. Overriding a veto takes three-fifths of each house's members: 25 representatives and 13 senators.
  8. The budget. The fiscal year starts July 1. The Joint Finance Committee writes the operating budget and the Joint Capital Improvement Committee the bond bill; both, with the grants-in-aid bill, pass in the last days before June 30.
  9. Constitutional amendments. In Delaware they're bills. "An act proposing an amendment" has to pass each house by two-thirds of its members; after an election, the next General Assembly has to pass "an act concurring in" the same amendment, unchanged, by two-thirds again. Then it's part of the constitution. Amendments never go to the governor, and Delaware is the only state where they don't go to the voters.
  10. The Lieutenant Governor. The Lieutenant Governor presides over the Senate and isn't a senator; the President Pro Tempore leads the Senate.

How to have an impact

  • Speak at the committee meeting. Committees take public comment on the bills on their agenda, in Legislative Hall in Dover or online. A bill needs the signatures of most of the committee's members to get out, so the members matter as much as the chair.
  • Ask the majority leader. A released bill waits on the ready list until the majority leader puts it on the agenda. Many bills that get out of committee never come up for a vote.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state senator and one state representative, and they listen most to their own constituents.
  • Mind June. Most bills pass in the session's last weeks, before June 30. Bills don't die at the end of the first year, so a bill that stalled can move in the second.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please support it in the Senate"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Delaware General Assembly's website, legis.delaware.gov (the list of bills and substitutes with their synopses, each bill's page, history, committee reports and roll calls with how each member voted, the members, committees and members' pages for phones and emails), and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, towns, communities and counties.

House and Senate bills are shown, including the bills that propose or concur in constitutional amendments; resolutions are left out. A bill replaced by a substitute is shown under its own number with the substitute's title, synopsis, sponsors and votes. Each bill's title is the subject in its long title ("relating to ..."), or the name of the act; its summary is the long title. Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to and words in its title, so a bill can have more than one.

"What it does" is the bill's synopsis, a plain explanation written with the bill and printed at its end; it isn't part of the law, and it describes the bill as filed (or the substitute), not later amendments.

Committees' votes are the counts of members who signed the report (favorable, on its merits, unfavorable); there are no names. Floor votes are roll calls, member by member.

Some legislators' pages no longer list a phone or email once their term has ended. Several representatives list their caucus's shared office line.

The records don't show when a bill is sent to the governor, so the governor's deadline isn't shown.

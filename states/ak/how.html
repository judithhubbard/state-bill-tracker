The steps

  1. Introduction. A legislator, a committee, or the Rules Committee at the governor's request files a bill. Each Legislature lasts two years (the 34th is 2025–26), with a regular session each year from the third Tuesday of January, up to 121 days, usually to mid-May. Bills that don't pass in the first year carry over to the second. The governor or two-thirds of the legislators can call special sessions, whose bills are numbered from 1001.
  2. Committees. The Speaker or the Senate President refers the bill to one or more committees, in order; bills that cost money usually end with Finance. Each committee's chair decides whether it gets a hearing, and bills are often "heard and held" several times. When a committee reports the bill, each member signs a recommendation: "do pass", "do not pass", "amend" or "no recommendation". The committee can rewrite the bill as a committee substitute (CS).
  3. Rules and the floor. After its last committee, the bill goes to the house's Rules Committee, which decides whether and when it goes on the calendar. On the floor the bill is read a second time, when amendments are offered, and a third time for the vote. It needs a majority of all members: 21 in the House, 11 in the Senate.
  4. The other house. The bill goes through committees and the floor again. If the second house changes it, the first house has to agree ("concur"); if it won't, a conference committee can write a compromise, which both houses have to adopt.
  5. The governor. Passed bills go to the governor when the Legislature transmits them, often weeks or months later. The governor then has 15 days, or 20 if the Legislature has adjourned (Sundays excepted), to sign or veto; a bill the governor doesn't act on becomes law. The governor can veto or reduce items in an appropriation bill. Laws are numbered as chapters of the session laws (Chapter 5, SLA 2025).
  6. Veto overrides. The House and Senate meet together in a joint session. Overriding takes two-thirds of all 60 legislators (40 votes), or three-fourths (45) for an appropriation bill. A veto issued after the Legislature adjourns is taken up within five days of its next session.
  7. Constitutional amendments. The Legislature proposes an amendment to the Alaska Constitution with a joint resolution (HJR or SJR). It skips the governor: two-thirds of each house's members send it to the voters at the next general election.
  8. Coalitions. Party seat counts don't say who runs each house. In 2025–26, bipartisan majority coalitions chose the Speaker and the Senate President and run the committees: Democrats, independents and two Republicans in the House; Democrats and Republicans in the Senate.

How to have an impact

  • Get the bill a hearing. Most bills that fail are never moved out of a committee. Ask your legislators and the bill's sponsors to press the chair, and write the committee members yourself.
  • Testify. Committees take public testimony in person in Juneau, by phone, and at Legislative Information Offices around the state. Hearings are streamed on akl.tv and Gavel Alaska.
  • Mind the Rules Committee. A bill that has cleared its committees still needs the Rules Committee to put it on the calendar. Ask the chair and your legislators.
  • Bring in your own legislators. You have one state representative and one state senator, and they listen most to their own constituents. Ask them to co-sponsor it and vote for it.
  • Write again only with something new, such as a new fact, a local impact, or a new stage ("it just passed the House; please hear it in the Senate"). The same message twice from the same person usually counts once.
  • Multiply people, not messages. Ten different people writing outweighs one person writing ten times.
  • Make it personal. A sentence about why it matters to you or your community carries more weight than form text.
  • Bills carry over. A bill that doesn't pass in the first year stays alive for the second, so there's time to build support between sessions.

About this data

Where the data comes from: the Alaska State Legislature's BASIS public data service (each bill's history, sponsors, committee referrals and reports, subjects, and how each member voted on the floor; the members and committees), and the Census Bureau's district relationship files for cities, communities, boroughs and census areas.

House and Senate bills are shown, including special sessions' bills, with the joint resolutions that propose constitutional amendments; other resolutions are left out. Each bill's title is its short title from the Legislature, and its summary the official title ("An Act relating to ..."). Topics come from the committees a bill was sent to, its subjects and words in its title, so a bill can have more than one.

There's no summary of what each bill does. The Legislature's site has each sponsor's statement, but those argue for the bill as it was introduced, so they aren't shown here; follow the link to the bill's page to read one. Committees don't take roll calls: their reports list each member's recommendation, shown in the bill's history.
